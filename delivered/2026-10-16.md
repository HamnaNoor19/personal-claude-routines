## 1. Anthropic says Claude worked out where the internet's standard encryption constants came from, in under 30 minutes
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-ecdsa-curve-origins [TODO: verify exact URL; the post (Frontier Red Team, Oct 16, 2026, "Claude reveals ECDSA curve origins") was read via WebFetch summary at a guessed URL, and the Anthropic research index lists it]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: capability demo / hands-on result. Angle: stakes-first, with the "not a break" caveat kept in the open. Frames not taken: security vulnerability story, AI-for-science hype.

For about 25 years, nobody could explain where the numbers behind the most widely used internet encryption curves came from, and that gap fed a suspicion that the NSA had hidden something in them.

Anthropic says one of its internal research models, with no hint about the answer, worked it out in under 30 minutes. The "seeds" that NIST published alongside these curves (the starting values the curve constants are derived from) were never hash outputs, as the standard implies. Per Anthropic, they came from a weak 1990s random number generator called lrand48, seeded with the computer's clock. Ten of the seeds map to dates between May 1997 and April 1998.

The method is the part I found most interesting. Claude guessed the software had used an old, weak generator, then tested 34 generator and slice combinations. Four seeds had their top bit switched off in every one of 20 words, a pattern with roughly a 1 in 5,810 chance, which matched lrand48 specifically. Brute force and a lattice-reduction technique recovered the generator's internal state, and the recovered states regenerated all 160 bits of each of the ten prime-curve seeds. Anthropic's code reproduces six published base points too.

What this does not show: it is not a break of the cryptography, and Anthropic itself says there is no action for anyone to take. Several things stay unexplained, including the base points for P-256 and the other large curves, and the explanation for the binary-curve dates is tentative. My read is that this makes a deliberate backdoor less plausible, not impossible. What stays with me is that a decades-old puzzle that bounties and expert attempts did not crack got a testable answer from hypothesis, scanner and check, not from a lucky guess.

## 2. Anthropic staff and the US public agree on what AI can do today. They disagree about 2030 by 91% to 39%.
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/ai-expectations-gap (primary, Oct 16, 2026, read via WebFetch summary) [TODO: verify figures against full text]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: AI discourse / survey result. Angle: skeptical pushback; find the strongest number in the survey and say who is measuring it.

Ask Americans and Anthropic employees which AI tasks are hardest, and they rank them in nearly the same order. Ask whether AI will make a Nobel-level discovery by 2030 and the answers split: 39% of US adults say yes, against 91% of Anthropic staff.

That is from "The AI expectations gap," an Anthropic report comparing a Morning Consult survey of 10,980 US adults (Aug 11-23) with an internal poll of 1,334 Anthropic staff. The disagreement is not about what AI can do now. It is about speed, and about how much of it reaches real workplaces. By 2030 the public expects AI to be doing 42% of the tasks it is capable of, staff expect 65%. Between 19% and 40% of US adults say AI will never manage a given task, versus at most 2% of staff.

The number I keep coming back to is on unemployment. People who expect AI to do under half of their own work forecast 7-8% unemployment in 2030, from a 4.2% baseline. People who expect it to do 80% or more forecast 12.2%. The authors say each extra 10 points of expected exposure goes with about 0.4 points more forecast unemployment, and that this holds for staff too. General optimism about AI did not predict it. So the fear seems to track "will this hit my job," not "is AI impressive."

I'd push back on reading staff as the correct answer. It is an internal, unweighted poll of people who work at a frontier lab, the public sample is an opt-in online panel, and the authors work for Anthropic. Time-saved and task-share figures are self-reported. The authors suggest public views may drift toward staff views as usage spreads, which is a guess. The measured gap is real. Who is closer to right in 2030 is not something this survey can say.

## 3. At Anthropic, junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9% as agents logged six times the hours of their human managers
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic (primary, Oct 16, 2026, "How employment is changing at Anthropic", read via WebFetch summary) [TODO: verify figures against full text and confirm final URL]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / internal-data report. Angle: stakes-first for early-career readers, with the self-study caveat stated plainly. Frames not taken: productivity-gains story, policy-proposals story.

If you are early in a technical career, one company has now published its own numbers on what happens to junior hiring when AI agents become routine: at Anthropic, juniors fell from 27% of technical hires to 9%.

The report, "How employment is changing at Anthropic," is written by four Anthropic economists and uses the company's own data. Junior staff went from 21% to 14% of technical employment since early 2025, and postings for juniors dropped sharply after December 2025. In a survey of managers in April 2026, about a third of ML managers and a fifth of software engineering managers said AI led them to hire less. Meanwhile, hours worked by employee-directed agents were six times the hours of the humans directing them by August, and code commits per engineer were around 5x what they were six months earlier.

Two details made it feel less like a victory lap. About 80% of interactive session time is spent waiting on a human, so attention, not model speed, is the bottleneck. And "meta-work" (building agent instructions, reviewing agent output, monitoring pipelines) went from 10% to 22% of sessions in six months.

Plenty of reasons to hold this loosely. It is one company, written by that company, and the authors say so. People who adopted coding agents were already on steeper output trajectories, so the comparison of 192 adopters with 74 non-adopters may overstate the effect. More commits is not more value. The authors also concede their proposed fixes (stock ownership, early warning, internal moves) do not address the slowdown in junior hiring directly. Still, this is rare: a lab publishing what its own AI adoption did to its own staffing.
