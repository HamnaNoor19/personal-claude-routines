## 1. Google DeepMind maps the road from AGI to ASI

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.12683
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Threat-model mapping. Angle: the physical limits argument is more interesting than the pathway taxonomy.

---

Frame: Threat-model / strategic landscape. Angle: personal-noticing — the physical limits argument stopped me cold, not the four pathways.

---

Google DeepMind just published a 60-page map of how AI could go from human-level to smarter-than-all-humans-combined. The most interesting part isn't the timeline speculation. It's the limits.

The paper is by Shane Legg (GDM co-founder, someone who spent years defining what AGI even means operationally) and Marcus Hutter (creator of AIXI, a formal theory of universal intelligence). It lays out four pathways from AGI to ASI — scaling what's already working, finding better algorithms, AI improving its own architecture, and large networks of AI instances coordinating together. They're careful to note these aren't competing bets; the actual path could involve all four.

My read is that this is more useful as a map of the territory than as a prediction. Nobody knows which combination gets us there, or when. What Legg and Hutter are doing is insisting we reason carefully about the shape of the problem before we need to.

What I keep returning to is the physical-limits argument. Even ASI would be constrained by the speed of light (no thinking faster than physics allows), by thermodynamics (no computation without heat), by Gödel's incompleteness results (there are true things no formal system can prove from inside itself), and by computational complexity (some problems are hard in ways no intelligence can overcome). These aren't hopeful safety arguments. They're just math.

Framing this as "DeepMind predicts superintelligence" misses what the paper actually does. It's a research program in slow motion: first define AGI (March 2026 paper), then approach its safety (April 2025 paper), then map what comes after. That deliberate sequence of documents is worth paying attention to on its own.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / agentic AI. Angle: frame-shift — this isn't a convenience story, it's an accountability design question.

---

Visa announced this week that ChatGPT can now complete purchases on your behalf — charge your card, place the order, without a confirm click from you. Most coverage is framing this as a convenience story. That framing buries what's actually interesting.

ChatGPT has been able to recommend products for a while. What changed is completing the transaction. The gap between "AI suggests" and "AI buys" is small in UI terms and large in accountability terms. A recommendation you can ignore. A purchase has a paper trail, a dispute window, and someone's card balance on the line.

From a design standpoint, the authorization question matters enormously: Did the user pre-approve any purchase under $X? In any category? With any merchant? The answer changes whether this is a productivity tool or a liability surface. I don't know yet what the authorization model looks like in the Visa-ChatGPT integration specifically [TODO: verify], but that design choice will matter more than the partnership announcement itself.

Worth watching secondarily: once Visa processes AI-initiated transactions at scale, Mastercard, Stripe, and every other payment processor faces the same capability question. The infrastructure for agent-driven commerce is either being built carefully right now or being normalized without the right specifications in place.

Whether this goes well depends on whether the authorization and reversal mechanisms are designed thoughtfully. That's the part nobody's publishing yet.
