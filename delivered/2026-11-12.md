## 1. Google DeepMind says AI agents proved that core parts of Signal's encryption code do what they promise, all the way down to machine code
**Source:** https://deepmind.google/blog/introducing-alphaproof-code/ (project page: https://google-deepmind.github.io/code-verification/; neither page could be fetched, details via search summaries and coverage such as The Register) [TODO: verify]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: capability demo / technique. Angle: niche concept made legible; what it means to prove a lock instead of testing it, and why the proof is only as good as the human-written definition of "secure." Format: LinkedIn.

Almost every piece of security software on your phone has been tested but not proven correct. Google DeepMind says its AI agents just proved that key parts of Signal's encryption code keep their promises, checked down to the instructions the processor actually runs.

The system is called AlphaProof Code, announced Nov 10. People first write down in precise mathematical terms what "secure" means for the code (the specification). AI agents then build a proof that the code meets it, and Lean (software that checks every logical step mechanically) has to accept the proof. The target was libsignal, the core library behind Signal's messaging. The proven properties include forward secrecy, meaning a key stolen today cannot unlock messages you sent yesterday. Coverage reports millions of lines of machine-checked proof (DeepMind's blog says over 3 million, other outlets say about 6.5 million) and says the agents flagged about 1% of the code for an extra human look. [TODO: verify figures against DeepMind's paper]

Testing is like trying a thousand lockpicks and finding none that work. A proof is closer to showing that no pick can work, given the assumptions you wrote down.

That last clause is where I would focus. The proof only covers what the specification says, so a spec that leaves something out still gets a valid proof. DeepMind says human experts reviewed every specification. It also says the full Signal app was not verified, and coverage reports no new vulnerabilities in the parts checked.

My speculation, not their finding: if AI makes proofs this cheap, verification could move from rare, high-stakes projects to routine for security libraries, and the scarce skill becomes writing the right spec.

## 2. Railway's breach report: attackers searched stolen app settings for AI keys specifically, and ran up an estimated $740,000 on them
**Source:** https://blog.railway.com/p/incident-report-october-security-incident (Railway's final incident report, dated Nov 10; not fetched directly, details via search excerpts and coverage such as eSecurity Planet https://www.esecurityplanet.com/cybersecurity/news-railway-breach-ai-api-keys-fraud-charges/) [TODO: verify]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news (security incident). Angle: stakes-first; an AI API key now behaves like a company credit card, and attackers are treating it that way. Format: LinkedIn.

If your app keeps an AI API key in a cloud host's settings, that key is now one of the first things an attacker goes looking for. The hosting company Railway's breach report shows why: the key is close to cash.

Railway published its final incident report on Nov 10. Its account: on Oct 20 an attacker used an unknown flaw to break out of the walled-off space where customer code gets built, then decrypted environment variables (the settings where apps store passwords and keys). Database logs show the attacker searching specifically for variable names that looked like AI provider keys. Coverage of the report puts the unauthorized AI usage at about $740,000, run up on Amazon Bedrock, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google Gemini. [TODO: verify the $740,000 figure in Railway's report] Railway believes the keys were resold or used for distillation (training a cheaper copy of a model on its answers). It initially told customers it could not refund the charges, then reversed course, and now says it will cover what the AI providers decline to refund.

Stolen secrets from hosting platforms are not new. The selection is what stands out to me. A database password needs more work before it pays off, while an AI key pays immediately, in compute someone else is billed for.

By the company's own count, exposure reached no more than 1.85% of workspaces, an upper bound rather than a confirmed count. If you build with these APIs, the practical move is boring and worth doing today: set spending limits on each key, give each app its own key, and rotate anything that ever sat in a shared dashboard.

## 3. New York opened registration for its frontier AI law, which will require the biggest labs to report serious incidents within 72 hours
**Source:** https://www.tokenpost.com/news/regulation/54396 (secondary; the NY Department of Financial Services announcement was not reached; see also https://fpf.org/blog/the-raise-act-vs-sb-53-a-tale-of-two-frontier-ai-laws/) [TODO: verify]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / policy (not alignment research or evals). Angle: stakes-first with a skeptical edge; registration is paperwork, but the incident-reporting clock is the part that could produce real information, and its definitions are still unsettled. Format: LinkedIn.

From Jan 1, the companies building the most powerful AI models will owe a New York regulator a report within 72 hours when something seriously goes wrong. The first step went live this week: the state opened a registration portal for them on Nov 10.

The law is the RAISE Act, and a new office inside New York's Department of Financial Services (the state's bank and insurance regulator) is running it. Reporting says it covers developers with more than $500 million in annual revenue that have trained models using more than 10^26 operations (a compute threshold only the largest training runs reach). Covered companies must register by Jan 1, 2027. They also must publish a safety framework, meaning a public document describing how they test for and limit serious risks, and report serious incidents within 72 hours, or within 24 hours to authorities when there is an imminent risk of death or serious injury. To be fair to the skeptics, most of this is disclosure. The law mostly makes companies say what they do rather than telling them what to do, and reported penalty amounts vary by source, with one tracker saying they were reduced in a March 2026 amendment. [TODO: verify]

I still think the 72-hour clock matters more than it looks. Today we mostly learn about AI failures when a lab chooses to publish them. A legal duty to report creates a record that does not depend on that choice. How useful that record is will depend on how narrowly "serious incident" gets defined, and the state has not said which companies have registered so far.
