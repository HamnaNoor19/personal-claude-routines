## 1. Anthropic interviewed 58 companies about AI at work: few layoffs, but two-thirds are hiring less
**Source:** Anthropic Economics (Wilmers, Massenkoff, McCrory), "Working with AI," published Nov 23, 2026, https://www.anthropic.com/research/working-with-ai (read directly)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / labor. Not taken: product launch, conceptual explainer. Angle: frame-shift. The likely headline is "AI isn't causing layoffs," but the number that matters for job seekers is the hiring slowdown. Format: LinkedIn.

Two-thirds of the 58 companies Anthropic's economists interviewed say AI has reduced their hiring. Only two said AI had fully eliminated a job. If you're looking for work right now, the first number matters more than the second.

The report, "Working with AI," came out today from Anthropic's economics team. It's built on 66 in-person interviews at US companies ranging from AI-native startups to century-old firms in law, dentistry, construction and trucking. Most people interviewed said AI pays off by letting them produce more, rather than by cutting costs.

Layoffs really were rare in this sample. Two firms saw AI fully replace a position, five pushed out people who refused to use it, and ten hired more because of AI. The squeeze shows up in other places. About a third of the hiring cuts came from simply not replacing people who left. Nine companies cut contractors or offshore staff in roles like customer service, legal and back-office work. Six AI-native startups estimated they would need four times as many employees without AI.

It's worth being clear about the limits. These are Anthropic's own business customers, picked for variety rather than at random, and the gains are self-reported rather than measured. The authors say both things themselves. A company that sells AI also has an obvious interest in the "more output, not fewer people" story, so I'd read this as a careful field report rather than a verdict.

My read is that AI's job effects may show up first as roles that never get posted. That barely shows up in layoff data, but it's easy to feel as a new grad sending out applications.

## 2. An AI coding agent leaked a key, and thieves downloaded customers' AI prompts, then searched them for passwords
**Source:** Together AI, "Security event: Prompt leakage for requests in June and July of 2026," Nov 21, 2026, https://www.together.ai/blog/security-event-prompt-leakage-for-requests-in-june-and-july-of-2026 (not reachable from this session; details via search excerpts of the post and coverage such as https://dev.to/tin_thnhbnh_6d6c9102d/an-ai-coding-agent-committed-the-keys-inside-together-ais-prompt-leak-439c and https://www.techbooky.com/together-ai-says-coding-agent-exposed-credential-in-customer-prompt-leak/) [TODO: verify details against Together's post]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security (not safety: the agent made an ordinary mistake, and the attack came from an outside party). Not taken: governance. Angle: stakes-first. Anything you paste into a prompt can end up stored somewhere, and here someone mined stored prompts for other people's passwords. Follows from the Dragos canary-token item in the Nov 21 digest.

If you've ever pasted a password or API key into an AI prompt, this one is for you. Together AI, which runs open AI models for other companies, says samples of its customers' prompts were stolen, after an AI coding agent leaked the key that protected them.

According to Together's disclosure on Nov 21, the agent was working on one of the company's public GitHub projects and committed a credential (a secret access key) into the public code. That key opened a private repository holding samples of customer prompts from June and July. The prompts had been sent to three open models (MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6) and kept for routine software testing. Together's scanning caught the leaked key and the commit was reverted, but someone had already downloaded the samples.

What happened next worries me more. The intruder searched the stolen prompts for logins to other services and tried them. Five of the credentials they tried were fake "canary" keys (decoys that alert their owner when used) that Dragos planted for the prompt-leak study it published two days earlier.

To be fair to the agent, accidentally committing a secret is one of the oldest human mistakes in software, and a standard scanner caught it. My read, which is speculation, is that the new part is volume: agents write and push a lot of code, and a leaked key only has to work for a short window. Together says it is still reviewing whether personal information was exposed. Its fix makes "zero data retention" (prompts aren't stored at all) the default for new accounts only, so existing customers have to turn it on themselves.

The practical lesson for everyone else: assume anything you paste into a prompt might be stored, and change any key or password you've already pasted.

## 3. Amazon caught AI agents finding an unknown flaw and taking over a university server in 11 minutes, after 16 hours of failing elsewhere
**Source:** Amazon Threat Intelligence (CJ Moses), AWS Security Blog, "Chinese-nexus actor's Cairn AI agents found a zero-day on their own," Nov 20, 2026, https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/security/chinese-nexus-actors-cairn-ai-agents-found-a-zero-day-on-their-own/ (not reachable from this session; details via search excerpts of the post and coverage such as https://www.securityweek.com/amazon-china-linked-hackers-ai-agents-exploited-medical-software-zero-day/ and https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-monai-label-agent-discovered-zero-day-2026/) [TODO: verify figures against Amazon's post]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security. Not taken: dangerous-capability evals, governance. Angle: frame-shift. Coverage leads with "11 minutes," but the same log shows agents that are tireless rather than brilliant, which makes basic security settings matter more. Builds on the Google GTIG item from the Nov 18 digest.

Amazon says AI agents found a previously unknown flaw in medical-imaging software and used it to take over a university server, going from first look to writing files on it in about 11 minutes. The less-quoted part of the same report is the 16 hours they spent failing.

The write-up, published Nov 20 by Amazon Threat Intelligence, explains how they saw all this. Its team found an exposed control server for Cairn, an open-source tool that runs AI agents to do penetration testing (authorized hacking to find weak spots). The server had no login, so Amazon could read the operator's instructions and the agents' output from a campaign against East Asian universities on Sept 8 to 10. Amazon attributes it to a China-linked actor with moderate confidence.

Their one big success came against MONAI Label, an open-source tool researchers use to label medical images. The agents found a zero-day (a flaw nobody had reported yet) in its file-upload feature, wrote files onto the server, and later got full administrator control. Amazon is highly confident the flaw was new, and the project has since released a fix. Coverage says MONAI Label ships with login turned off by default. [TODO: verify]

Elsewhere, the agents spent about 16 hours on a university database server without getting in. They also tried more than 30,000 username and password combinations on a GPU server, and none worked.

My read is that these agents come across as tireless and cheap more than clever. They keep trying until something left unlocked gives way. That's worrying, especially a week after Google reported attackers using AI to turn security patches into working attacks. It also means that boring defenses like logins and keeping research tools off the open internet still decide who gets hit.

If you run research software on a university network, today is a good day to check what answers the internet without asking for a password.
