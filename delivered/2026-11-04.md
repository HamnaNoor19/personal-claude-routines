## 1. Claude helps solve the structure of a protein shell biologists have studied since 1986
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-vault-particles-with-claude
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability demo (science) / stakes-first: the speedups came from the boring middle steps, and the write-up is unusually frank about Claude's costly mistakes. Format: LinkedIn.

Anthropic's science team says Claude helped work out the 3D structure of a cell part that biologists have known about since 1986 and still can't explain. The project took under four months.

The part is the vault particle: a large, hollow protein capsule found in most animal cells. Nobody has confirmed what it does. Mice without vaults survive, which is part of why the question has stayed open, and vaults are missing from the usual lab organisms (yeast, flies, worms), so progress has been slow.

Here is what Anthropic's Life Sciences team reports. Claude searched about 15 billion predicted protein sequences for relatives of the vault protein. Of 24 candidates expressed in E. coli, 15 gave soluble protein, 7 assembled into vault-like particles, and cryo-EM (imaging frozen molecules to reconstruct their 3D shape) confirmed four. The one they studied most, from a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria), is built from 96 protein copies versus 78 in human vaults. Purification dropped from roughly three days to about 90 minutes, and a first structural model was ready in 34 hours.

What I found more persuasive than the speedups was the list of failures, which the team published themselves. Claude wrote a protocol with a growth-medium additive that interferes with DNA uptake, costing about two weeks. It also skipped a standard cryo-EM step and produced a map blended with its own mirror image, which people had to catch. The authors say the errors were costliest in areas where the team lacked expertise.

My read, and it is speculative: the gains here sit in the tedious middle of a project, and they seem to depend on someone who can tell when the output is wrong. Also worth holding in mind: this is Anthropic reporting on its own tool, the cryo-EM step used hundreds of GPUs, the vaults were only made in E. coli, and what vaults actually do is still unknown. ## 2. Anthropic's FlashProve: a cryptographic receipt for "this model wrote this answer"
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security / governance. Stakes-first: today you cannot check which model answered you; here is a working prototype and exactly how far it is from usable. Format: LinkedIn.

When you pay for a top-tier AI model through a middleman, you currently have no way to check that the answer came from that model and not a cheaper one.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team (Rinberg, Allain et al., with academic collaborators) published a prototype called FlashProve that tries to fix this. It attaches a zkSNARK to a response: a short cryptographic proof that a specific, publicly committed set of model weights produced exactly these output tokens, checkable without seeing the weights. Think of a tamper-evident seal, not a watermark.

The numbers are what make it interesting, and also what keep it a prototype. On Llama 4 Maverick (a 400B-parameter open-weights model), proving costs about 72 times the GPU time of just serving the same requests, each proof is about 18.8 MB, and checking one takes roughly 17 seconds on a CPU. For comparison, the post cites an earlier system that needed 14.3 hours to prove 1,000 tokens of the same model.

The limits are stated plainly by the authors. Proofs only cover windows up to 1,024 tokens. They assume greedy decoding (always picking the most likely token) on an exact integer version of the weights. Proving happens after generation, not live. And a proof shows which model served a request, not what else the hardware was used for.

I find it compelling that the formal pieces are written in Lean (a proof-checking language), so an auditor can check the claims rather than trust them. I can't judge the cryptography myself. My speculation is that if this ever gets cheap, it becomes plumbing for regulators and API customers who need to know what actually ran. Today, at 72x overhead, it is a research result.
