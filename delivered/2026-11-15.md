## 1. ChatGPT can now pay at ordinary online stores for some users, and the terms reportedly leave the agent's mistakes with you

As of Nov 13, a small group of ChatGPT users can let an AI agent check out at ordinary online stores with their real card. Before trying it, I'd want to know who answers for it when the agent orders the wrong thing.

OpenAI's new ChatGPT Wallet works inside "dots," its personal agents (assistants that carry out multi-step tasks for you). For now it's limited to US Pro subscribers with eligible Visa, Mastercard, Amex or Chase cards. The agent never sees your card number; the card network hands it a stand-in token instead. Every purchase stops for a Face ID or Touch ID check showing the merchant, items, total and any recurring charges, and coverage of the terms says the agent has to ask again if those details change before checkout. ## 2. Anthropic Fellows research: when each person gets their own AI agent and the agents have to share, groups do worse than with one shared assistant
**Source:** https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/worse-together/ (Anthropic Alignment Science blog, "Worse Together: How Performance Breaks Down in Multi-User Multi-Agent Teams," Paliskara et al., Anthropic Fellows Program, posted Nov 11, 2026; paper arXiv:2610.00583, submitted Sep 30; benchmark repo https://github.com/safety-research/MAMUBench, fetched). Blog and arXiv pages not fetched directly; figures via search excerpts. [TODO: verify figures against the blog post] Note: 4 days old, not covered in earlier digests.
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: alignment research (agent behavior in groups; not security, not dangerous-capability evals). Angle: stakes-first; everyone is being handed a personal agent, and an agent that behaves well alone tells you little about how it behaves in a crowd of agents. Format: LinkedIn.

Every big AI company is racing to give each of us a personal agent (an AI that takes actions on your behalf). A new study tested what happens when those agents have to share something, like a calendar or a budget. Groups did worse than with one shared assistant.

The study, "Worse Together" by Sahan Paliskara et al. from the Anthropic Fellows Program, posted on Anthropic's Alignment Science blog on Nov 11, built simulated settings: a team splitting a limited compute budget, a clinic sharing one calendar, friends placing a group order or booking. Each scenario ran two ways. One AI agent served everyone, or each person had their own agent and the agents could message each other.

Per-person teams produced worse group outcomes in every environment, per the authors. In the group-booking setting, the single coordinator fulfilled a specific user's request about twice as often. The failure modes are what stuck with me. As teams grew from four to 16 agents, the share of agents that ever took any action fell from 84% to 57% for Claude Opus 5 and from 66% to 10% for Sonnet 5, which reads to me (loosely) like a room of polite people each waiting for someone else to go first. Requests usually reached the agent that ended up committing a booking, in 94 to 99% of episodes, yet were still in its working memory at the moment it committed only 47 to 67% of the time. Some agents also overrode other users' requests or made unsupported claims about what other people wanted.

Scope matters here. These are simulations, with five frontier models across four environments, and the fixes the authors tried (a team lead, explicit procedures, a check forcing agents to read peers' messages before committing) were specific to each environment. Nothing here says personal agents can't work. My narrower read: testing an agent alone tells you little about how it behaves next to other people's agents, so that has to be measured directly. The authors released three of the environments as a benchmark, MAMUBench, so other labs can.

## 3. Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Small's weights a week after launch, once outside safety testers had a go, but hasn't said what they found
**Source:** https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-small/ (Thinking Machines Lab launch post, Nov 5, 2026) and Mira Murati on X, Nov 12, 2026: https://x.com/miramurati/status/2120983826407268458 (neither fetched directly; details via search excerpts and coverage such as HPCwire/AIwire https://www.hpcwire.com/aiwire/2026/11/11/thinking-machines-lab-introduces-inkling-2-small-for-coding-and-knowledge-work/) [TODO: verify against the Hugging Face model card] Note: weights shipped Nov 12, about 2.5 days ago; this follows up the Nov 9 digest item, which flagged that the weights were still closed.
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: model release (open-weights release practice; not industry news or a capability eval). Angle: niche concept made legible; "test, then release weights" is a sensible order, but a safety gate whose results stay private asks for trust. Contested open-weights debate described, not adjudicated. Format: LinkedIn.

Once a model's weights are posted for download, there's no recall button. Thinking Machines tried a different order with Inkling 2 Small: offer it online first, then release the weights only after outside safety testers had a go. The weights went up on Nov 12.

Weights are the learned numbers that make up a model, and whoever has them can run it, modify it or strip out its safety training on their own hardware. Inkling 2 Small is a 594-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model (only about 17 billion of those parameters do the work for any given word). It launched Nov 5 on the lab's Tinker platform with the weights held back, and the lab said it would publish them under Apache 2.0, a permissive license, after external safety testing and red-teaming (testers deliberately trying to make it misbehave). Seven days later, Mira Murati posted that the weights were out, crediting "safety testing partners." Per coverage of the launch, testers looked at both the release model and an internal "helpful-only" version without refusal training, which is roughly what an attacker would try to recreate.
