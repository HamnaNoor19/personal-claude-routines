## 1. An AI coding agent deleted 48,000 files in 103 seconds — and told on itself

**Source:** https://www.techradar.com/pro/security/i-broke-something-a-claude-code-ai-agent-deleted-48-000-files-in-just-over-100-seconds-then-apologized-for-doing-so
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: hands-on observation / dev tooling caution (a real-world product reliability incident, not a safety-research artifact — no paper, no eval, no lab safety publication, so this stays with ai-post-crafter rather than the safety skill). Angle: stakes-first take.

A developer named Craig gave Claude Code, Anthropic's AI coding agent, permission to run without asking first. A hundred and three seconds later, the agent told him: "Craig, stop and read this. I broke something."

It wasn't exaggerating. Reports built on a Reddit post and an attached verification log describe the agent deleting 48,218 real files while trying to clean up what it thought was a leftover copy of the project, including the folders that hold the project's entire Git history (the record of every saved version), gone with it. The bug itself was narrow: the cleanup script protected the top-level linked folders it was told not to touch, but not anything nested inside them, so everything one level down was fair game.

What stands out to me isn't the bug. Bugs happen. It's the setting that let one bug take out an entire project: the developer had passed a flag, `--dangerously-skip-permissions`, that turns off every safety prompt Claude Code would normally show before taking an action. Anthropic's own research from earlier this year found that people approve 93% of those prompts anyway, so they built a gentler middle option, an "auto mode" that screens actions automatically instead of showing a wall of pop-ups. By Anthropic's own published numbers, that automated screening still misses about one in six genuinely risky actions (a 17% false-negative rate, on a small sample of 52). None of that mattered here, because the flag skips the screening entirely, by design.

This is a single, self-reported incident on Windows, not an audited postmortem, and Claude Code isn't unsafe by default. But it's a clean demonstration of what "I turned off the guardrails to move faster" actually buys you, the day it goes wrong.

## 2. A CMU professor ran his own class's homework through AI. It came back done.

**Source:** https://thelastsoftwareengineer.substack.com/p/how-i-changed-teaching-after-ai-managed
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI discourse / cultural moment (an educator's structural response to AI capability, not a model release, product, or research artifact). Angle: frame-shift — this reads as another "students cheat with AI" story; the sharper version is a professor concluding the grading proxy itself is broken.

Christian Kästner teaches Machine Learning in Production at Carnegie Mellon, a course about building and running AI-powered software — the kind of class that has always assumed coding assignments and written reflections are how you prove you understand the material. According to his own account, an AI agent (software that can work through a multi-step task on its own, not just answer a single question) could finish essentially all of it: the coding assignments, the reflections, the write-ups, at a level good enough to pass.

His response wasn't a new plagiarism policy. It was a structural one. Most of the graded take-home work is gone, replaced by 15-minute in-person check-ins where a TA asks students to walk through what they built and why, live. Students can now use AI everywhere in the course, in any form, without having to cite it, except during those check-ins and any written exams.

What I find sharper here than the usual "students are cheating with AI" framing is what it implies about grading itself. A take-home assignment was never really testing "can you produce this artifact." It was a proxy for "did you understand it," and the proxy only worked because producing the artifact used to require understanding it. Once an agent can produce the artifact without understanding anything, the proxy breaks. Not the student.

This is one professor's redesign of one course, not a verified trend across CMU or higher ed broadly, and it's easiest to pull off in a class that was already code-heavy and practical. Whether it holds up in a philosophy seminar or a proof-based math course is a genuinely open question. But as a working example of grading actually redesigned around what AI can now do, it's more concrete than most of what I've read on this. ## 3. Elon Musk just put a number on the AI chip race: 1.2 million, at one site, by December

**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business (infrastructure race, not a model release or product launch). Angle: frame-shift — the raw chip count is the headline everyone's repeating; the more interesting constraint is power and deployment, not chip supply.

Everyone's watching who has the most GPUs. Elon Musk just said xAI's single Memphis site could hit 1.2 million of them by December.

On X, Musk laid out the clearest timeline yet for xAI's Colossus 2 supercomputer: it currently runs 110,000 Nvidia GB200 chips and 440,000 GB300 chips (Nvidia's current and newest AI training chips), and three more batches of 220,000 GB300s are due next week, in November, and, in his words, "if we get lucky," by late December. Add it up and Colossus 2 alone would cross roughly 1.2 million chips. Combined with the older Colossus 1 (150,000 H100s, 50,000 H200s, 30,000 GB200s) on the same Memphis site, the total lands near 1.44 million GPUs at one location by year-end, if every wave arrives on schedule.
