## 1. Anthropic says a rival lab ran 151 million fake conversations through Claude to copy how it thinks

**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/threat-intelligence-report-september-2026
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security. Frames not taken: governance (the policy angle belongs to item 2), capability evals (no benchmark claim here). Angle: frame-shift — this is being covered as "Anthropic accuses Chinese labs of cheating," but the sharper story is what it reveals about commercial AI APIs as an attack surface between competitors, and how blurry "distillation" already is as a practice.

---

A rival AI company allegedly signed up as a paying Claude customer roughly 3,500 times over, and used those accounts to hold more than 151 million conversations with the model in three months.

That's the headline number from a threat intelligence report Anthropic published this week: accounts it traced to Alibaba, running at a peak of nearly 3 million exchanges a day between May and July, aimed at a specific target. Not chatting with Claude for its own sake, but capturing how it answers coding questions, works through multi-step logic, and plans agentic tasks (breaking a goal into steps and executing them), then using those transcripts to train Alibaba's own Qwen models. Anthropic calls it the largest "distillation" campaign it has ever documented; distillation just means training a new model on an existing one's outputs instead of from scratch.

Separately, Anthropic named Moonshot AI, maker of the Kimi chatbot, for something more direct: routing live Kimi user conversations through Claude behind the scenes, then serving Claude's answers back to customers as Kimi's own.

None of this is clean-cut theft. Distillation from a competitor's outputs sits in a legal and ethical gray zone the field has never fully settled, and plenty of open-weight models trace their lineage to exactly this kind of training. What's different here is the scale and the disguise: not a research team testing a rival's model, but thousands of accounts built to pass as ordinary customers while quietly reverse-engineering a competitor's product.

Worth remembering that Anthropic is both the one making this claim and the one who lost the business from it, so the framing isn't neutral. But if the numbers hold, an AI API's login page has become a place competitors go to study each other, not just a place customers go to get work done.

## 2. OpenAI wants to slow down. It's asking Congress whether that's even legal.

**Source:** https://decrypt.co/377990/openai-congress-ai-slowdown-legal
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Governance / policy. Frames not taken: alignment research (no technical artifact here, this is a policy and internal-stance story), AI security (no attack or vulnerability involved). Angle: frame-shift — most coverage reads this as "OpenAI has a change of heart on safety," the sharper detail is that the obstacle to caution isn't willpower, it's antitrust law written for a different era.

---

Sam Altman told OpenAI staff this week that the company is open to slowing down how fast it pushes frontier AI forward, and said he hopes other labs will do the same. That's a real reversal for a company whose founding argument was roughly: if we don't build it fast, someone less careful will.

The more interesting detail sits one layer down. Per reporting this week, OpenAI has also asked members of Congress whether a coordinated slowdown among AI labs would even be legal, specifically whether it would count as the kind of competitor coordination the Sherman Antitrust Act exists to stop. OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, had already written publicly that he wants "voluntary slowdowns to become commonplace" across the field. So the obstacle here isn't just willpower. It might be a law written for oil cartels, now standing in the way of AI labs agreeing not to race each other off a cliff.

OpenAI is separately calling for binding, capability-based national safety rules, a shift from its past preference for lighter-touch, voluntary standards.

Two honest caveats. First, Altman was explicit that not every lab would agree to slow down, so this isn't a plan, it's an opening bid. Second, a company calling for mandatory rules right as it sits near the front of the pack is also a company that can afford to comply with them in ways smaller competitors can't; that doesn't make the safety concern fake, but it's worth naming rather than taking the announcement at face value.

Whether this becomes real coordination or stays a talking point probably depends on whether a rival lab says yes in public before the news cycle moves on.

## 3. Nearly 1 in 10 exposed AI gateways still use the tutorial's example password

**Source:** https://thehackernews.com/2026/09/nearly-1-in-10-exposed-litellm-gateways.html
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Dev tooling / security. Frames not taken: industry news (this is a vulnerability finding, not a business move), model release (no model involved at all). Angle: skeptical pushback — while the industry worries about clever jailbreaks and prompt injection, the more common way into an AI system this week was a lot more boring.

---

Everyone's watching for AI to get jailbroken by a clever prompt. Meanwhile, the more common way into an AI system this week turned out to be a lot more boring: the front door was left unlocked with the key still hanging in it.

Security firm Wiz scanned the public internet for exposed LiteLLM servers (LiteLLM is a popular open-source "gateway," the piece of software companies put between their apps and the AI providers they pay, like OpenAI or Anthropic, so they can swap models without rewriting code). Of roughly 3,074 LiteLLM gateways it found openly reachable, close to 1 in 10 still accepted "sk-1234" as the master administrator key. That's not a guessed password. It's the literal placeholder value printed in LiteLLM's own setup instructions, and as of this week, it's still sitting there in the docs.

With that one key, Wiz found it could pull out every model-provider API key stored on the box, read live prompts and responses passing through it, reach any tools connected over MCP (the protocol that lets AI models call outside tools), and in its tests, go further and grab the cloud account's own credentials for the machine the gateway runs on.

None of this required breaking anything. Roughly 191 of the exposed gateways had no key set at all, and the rest simply never changed the one in the tutorial. This is the oldest security mistake there is, the equivalent of a hotel using the same universal key for every room, just wearing an AI wrapper. The industry spends a lot of energy on whether models can be tricked into saying something they shouldn't. This finding is a reminder that the more immediate risk, right now, for a lot of companies is whether anyone bothered to read past step one of the setup guide.
