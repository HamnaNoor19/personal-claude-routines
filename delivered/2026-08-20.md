## 1. OpenAI and Anthropic just split on whether AI vendors should keep your data

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/offering-zero-data-retention-for-frontier-models/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: this is being read as OpenAI out-privacying Anthropic; the sharper read is that neither company has resolved the actual trade-off, they've just picked different points on it.

---

OpenAI and Anthropic just staked out opposite positions on a question that matters more than any benchmark: when you send data to their API, does the company keep a copy of it?

On Wednesday, OpenAI previewed Private Safety Processing, a system meant to catch misuse (fraud, abuse, that kind of thing) across a customer's API calls without OpenAI staff ever seeing the actual prompts or responses. The pitch is that it sends only a narrow "something looks wrong here" signal, while the underlying content stays on the customer's own infrastructure or encrypted with keys only the customer controls. Zero data retention, the practice of the vendor keeping no copy at all, preserved.

Anthropic moved the opposite direction the same week. Its most capable models will now require enterprise customers to retain 30 days of logs, a change from the earlier setup where Anthropic held that data itself. The shift is that customers can now host those logs on their own cloud instead of Anthropic's, so a copy still exists, it just isn't Anthropic's copy anymore. Databricks and Microsoft, OpenAI's own investor, are already piloting it.

Most coverage is reading this as OpenAI trying to out-privacy Anthropic. I think that undersells it. Catching misuse requires looking at something, and looking at nothing means missing real abuse; what both companies have done is pick a different point on that trade-off and bet enterprises will prefer theirs. The detail that makes this interesting isn't the press release, it's that Microsoft is testing its closest competitor's retention model instead of waiting on its own.

Both are pilots, not shipped products. OpenAI's technical white paper isn't out until September, so how well "narrow signal, no content" holds up against a real misuse case is still unverified. Anthropic's version still means a log exists somewhere, just under the customer's control instead of the vendor's.

## 2. A solo developer got a GPT-2-sized model to autocomplete piano, live, on an iPhone

**Source:** https://simedw.com/2026/08/20/midi-autocomplete/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Hands-on observation — personal-noticing on someone else's build log: the interesting part isn't that it works, it's what got it there wasn't scale.

---

A solo developer trained a piano autocomplete model roughly the size of GPT-2, OpenAI's 2019 model, and got it running live, entirely on an iPhone, no server involved.

The project is called RollTab. Connect a MIDI piano (a digital piano that sends note data instead of raw audio) to your phone, play a few notes, and a 125-million-parameter model predicts what comes next, the same idea as GitHub Copilot's code suggestions, except the autocomplete is notes instead of text. It runs the whole thing on-device.

What's notable, per the developer's write-up, is that the gains didn't come from a bigger model. They came from three unglamorous things: finding a better way to encode the raw MIDI data so patterns were easier for a small model to learn, cleaning the training data aggressively, and adding a DPO pass at the end (a training step that nudges a model toward outputs a person would prefer, the same family of technique labs use to align chatbots). On an iPhone 15, the result plays back at around 108 notes a second, comfortably faster than a person can play.

Every frontier lab's marketing tells you scale is the lever. This is one build log from one hobby project, so it doesn't overturn that. But it's a clean reminder that "real-time, on-device, and actually good at one narrow thing" is a different design target than "as capable as possible," and for a lot of the AI features people would actually use day to day, that's the target that matters. It's free right now if you own a MIDI keyboard and an iPhone or iPad, so it's still a hobby project rather than a company.

## 3. Google is paying for AI chip diversification partly in stock, and that's the real story

**Source:** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-19/marvell-gives-google-right-to-buy-up-to-12-2-billion-in-shares
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: coverage is framing this as Marvell landing a huge customer; the payment structure is really Google hedging its own supply chain in public.

---

Marvell didn't just win a chip contract from Google this week. Google handed over up to $12.2 billion in stock warrants as part of the payment, and that detail says more than the deal itself.

The headline, per Bloomberg and CNBC: Google expanded its custom chip agreement with Marvell to cover AI inference accelerators (the chips that generate a model's answers once it's already trained, distinct from the chips used to train it) plus storage and networking controllers, all built to plug into Google's own TPU (its custom AI chip) lineup. If Google hits its purchase targets, Marvell could earn over $120 billion from the relationship by 2033.

Google isn't just paying cash for chips here. It's giving Marvell warrants, the right to buy Marvell stock later at a fixed price, worth up to $12.2 billion: roughly 59 million shares at about $207 each, vesting in chunks tied to every $500 million of chips Google actually buys. Marvell's stock jumped nearly 10% on the news. Broadcom, Google's existing chip partner and the company this deal is quietly hedging against, dropped more than 5%.

Most coverage is framing this as Marvell landing a huge customer. The more interesting read is what the payment structure says about Google's position. Handing a supplier equity upside, not just a purchase order, is what a buyer does when it needs the supplier to prioritize it over everyone else asking for the same chips. That's a company hedging its own supply chain, in public, with real numbers attached, at a moment when AI chip capacity is tight enough that even Google apparently won't just place an order and wait its turn.

The $120 billion figure is a ceiling tied to purchase milestones Google hasn't hit yet, not a signed check, and this doesn't replace Broadcom, who Google still relies on for its core TPU production. It's Google diversifying the parts around the chip, not swapping out the chip itself.
