## 1. A former OpenAI researcher says six Chinese AI models carry the fingerprint of the Nvidia chips the US banned from export to China
**Source:** https://unemploymentlabs.com/checkpoint-forensics (Will DePue, Unemployment Labs, "Top Chinese Frontier AI Models Carry the Fingerprint of Export-Controlled NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs," Nov 12, 2026; not fetched directly, details via search excerpts and coverage such as The Register https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/13/chinese-ai-labs-are-training-models-on-nvidias-blackwell-despite-export-controls-says-ex-openai-engineer/5309891) [TODO: verify figures against the report]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / policy (not model release). Angle: niche concept made legible; a model's publicly downloadable file can testify about the hardware it was born on, which turns open weights into evidence. Format: LinkedIn.

US export rules are meant to keep Nvidia's newest AI chips out of Chinese labs. A former OpenAI researcher says the proof of whether that's working may be sitting in files anyone can download: the published weights (the learned numbers that make up a model) of six Chinese AI models.

The trick is clever. Before training starts, every model is filled with random starting numbers, and the exact pattern of that randomness depends on how many processing cores the chip that generated it has. Nvidia's newest Blackwell chips have 148 or 152 of those cores, depending on the version. The older Hopper chips China could buy have 132. Will DePue (Unemployment Labs, Nov 12) searched more than six trillion combinations of chip layout and random seed to see which hardware could reproduce the starting numbers. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash, a Blackwell B300 setup matched 445 of 469 sampled rows exactly, per coverage of the report; Nvidia's H100 and AMD's MI300X matched none. [TODO: verify 445/469] He reports similar Blackwell signatures in models from DeepSeek, Moonshot (Kimi), MiniMax and Ant Group, while the same labs' earlier models, and the newest from Qwen, Zhipu and others, read as Hopper.

DePue's report is careful about its limits, and I want to be too. It identifies the chip that generated the starting weights, not where that chip was, who owned it, or whether any rule was broken. Blackwell systems can be rented in data centers outside China. The estimate of roughly 12,000 Blackwell GPUs (range 7,000 to 20,000) rests on assumptions DePue himself calls rough, and the analysis is self-published, not peer-reviewed. My read, and it's speculation: if this holds up, export enforcement just gained a new kind of audit trail, and labs gained a reason to scrub it. Either way, "open weights" now discloses more than the model.

## 2. OpenAI is training its models on jailbreaks that work every time, not the ones that get lucky once
**Source:** https://alignment.openai.com/fuzzy-robustness (OpenAI Alignment blog, "Improving Fuzzy Robustness in LLM Safety with Automated Red Teaming," Nov 12, 2026; not fetched directly, details via search excerpts) [TODO: verify against the post]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security (jailbreak resistance of an aligned model; the straddle with alignment training is named in the post). Not alignment research or evals. Angle: niche concept made legible; the hardest safety lines are the blurry ones, and a "reliable attack" is better training data than a lucky one. Format: LinkedIn.

The safety rules AI chatbots struggle with most aren't the obvious ones. "How do I make a bomb" is easy to refuse. "How does this piece of malware spread" could come from a defender or an attacker, and the model has to judge. OpenAI's alignment team just described how it's hardening models on exactly that blurry ground, and the detail I keep thinking about is a small one.

They set one AI model loose as an attacker. It starts from a seed request and keeps rewording it until the defending model gives an answer it shouldn't, which is what a jailbreak (wording that talks a model past its safety rules) is. The winning attacks become training data so the defender learns to hold the line. The twist is a rule OpenAI calls worst@n: an attack only counts if it succeeds across repeated tries, not on one lucky roll of the dice. Their argument, as I read it, is that one-off successes are mostly noise, and training on noise doesn't teach a model much.

OpenAI reports results for cyber, biology and violence-related requests, tested two ways: canned jailbreaks from an attacker model the defender never trained against, and a live attacker that can keep probing. Coverage of the post says training on this data improved robustness on both. One figure circulating with it: on fixed cyber jailbreaks, GPT-6 Astra responded safely about 91.5% of the time versus about 59% for GPT-5.6 Sol. [TODO: verify; this may come from the GPT-6 system card rather than this post, and the two models differ in more than this training]

Two honest caveats. These are OpenAI's own evaluations of OpenAI's own models, and they measure the model's replies, not the full set of filters running in the real product. And a better score against AI-generated attackers doesn't tell us how it fares against a determined human with a weekend.

Still, "only learn from attacks that reliably work" is a simple idea that probably travels well beyond one lab.

## 3. Google's new everyday Gemini model costs twice as much per word, and Google says it's cheaper per job
**Source:** https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-hydrogen/ (Google launch post, Nov 12, 2026; not fetched directly, details via search excerpts and coverage such as MarkTechPost https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/11/13/google-gemini-4-hydrogen/ and Google Cloud docs https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/gemini/4-hydrogen) [TODO: verify]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: model release / capability evaluation (not product launch or explainer). Angle: frame-shift; coverage leads with "pricier," but the number that matters for anyone building on it is cost per finished task, and that one is still unsettled. Format: LinkedIn.

Most people never use a company's flagship AI model. They use the fast, cheap tier running behind search boxes and free apps, and Google just swapped its version: Gemini 4 Hydrogen replaces Gemini 3.8 Flash in the Gemini app, Google Search and the developer API, as of Nov 12.

Most coverage leads with the price. Developers pay $1.50 per million tokens (chunks of text, roughly three-quarters of a word each) going in and $7.50 per million coming out, double Flash's $0.75 and $3.75. In return, Artificial Analysis, an independent benchmarking firm, scores Hydrogen 51 on its intelligence index versus 31 for Flash. Google's own numbers say it finishes tasks 2.5 times faster and scores 57% on Terminal-Bench 4.1 (a test of doing real work at a command line) versus 18.2% for Flash.
