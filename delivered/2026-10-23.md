## 1. PHP2Shell: AI-assisted researchers found two critical PHP bugs, and attackers were probing before the patch
**Source:** https://wiz.io/blog/php2shell-php-rce (Wiz write-up, per search summary; page unreachable this run) plus https://thenextweb.com/news/php2shell-php-rce-ai-openai-wiz [TODO: verify everything below against the Wiz post and the PHP advisories]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security (AI used offensively/defensively on ordinary software, not an attack on an AI system). Angle: stakes-first, with a hard line between what was reported and what it implies. Frames not taken: governance/policy, capability eval.

If your website runs on PHP, and a large share of the web does, it needed an emergency update this week. The unusual part is how the bugs were found: with AI help.

Per Wiz's write-up as relayed by press coverage, researchers at Calif, Wiz and OpenAI used AI tools to find two memory-safety bugs in PHP itself (CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, each rated 9.2 on the CVSS v4 severity scale). Wiz says every PHP version back to 5.4 is affected by at least one. Patched releases (8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, 8.2.35) shipped on October 21. Coverage says the NSA advised sites that couldn't patch immediately to go offline, and that Cloudflare reported early attacks. One report says details leaked before the fix and probing began about four days early. [TODO: verify the leak timeline, the "70% of websites" figure, and each company's exact role; sources disagree]

What this shows, narrowly: AI-assisted research can turn up severe flaws in very old, heavily reviewed code. What it might imply, and this is my speculation: if defenders can do this, so can attackers, and the gap between a bug being found and a bug being exploited may keep shrinking. Nothing I read measures that.

I haven't read the technical write-up myself, so I can't say how much of the finding was the AI and how much was the humans steering it. That split is the part I'd want to see before drawing a conclusion.

## 2. Big US banks are shutting the door on outside AI agents (and one is writing the liability rules first)
**Source:** https://cybernews.com/security/jpmorgan-chase-blocks-outside-ai-agents/ and https://digg.com/ai/c61euovc (secondary coverage via search; primary CNBC/Bloomberg/FT reporting not read) [TODO: verify all bank-specific claims against primary reporting]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business. Angle: stakes-first, framed around who is liable when an AI acts on your behalf. Frames not taken: product launch, discourse.

Suppose you tell an AI agent to move your savings to the account with the best rate. If it makes a mistake, who pays? Several big US banks appear to have decided they don't want to find out yet.

From the coverage I could find: JPMorgan's CEO said outside agents would not be allowed to log in with customers' banking credentials, though some can read balances through connectors like Plaid. Citi reportedly bars third-party agents from logging in or transacting, with limited read-only access possible via open-banking providers. Bank of America is described as taking a conditional approach, limiting agents' access to consumer deposits while it writes protocols for liability on AI-authorized payments. Schwab's CEO said it blocks large unauthorized agents "in the tens and 20s" each week. [TODO: verify each attribution and the Schwab quote; I could not find the Axios piece that prompted this]

The pattern is read-only yes, money-moving no. A reason cited in coverage is also less noble than security: agents could shift idle deposits to higher-yielding accounts. That is reporting about motives, and I can't tell from here how much weight it carries compared with fraud and liability.

My read is that the interesting number will be how long "no" lasts. Banks that hold the customer relationship can set the terms, but customers who want the convenience may route around them.
