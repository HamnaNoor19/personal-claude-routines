## 1. Anthropic Economics: how 207,049 research sessions show scientists use Claude
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-researchers-use-claude
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Stakes-first / skeptical-leaning: scientists hand AI the work it is already good at, and physical lab work caps the speedup. Format: LinkedIn. Published Nov 5 (inside the 48h window).

Computer science is 4.3% of the world's researchers, but 32.7% of the research sessions on Claude Code. Anthropic's Economics team just measured how scientists actually use its AI, and the picture is less "AI scientist" and more "AI assistant for the middle of the project."

They labeled 207,049 research sessions from Aug-Sep 2026 on consumer plans and compared them with how researchers say they spend their time. Data analysis is 33.7% of Claude chat sessions versus 27.6% of researcher time. Writing is 32.0% versus 16.0%. Gathering data is 11.5% versus 28.6%. Ideation, the part most of the hype is about, is 4.2% of sessions versus 7.1% of time. In a survey of 528 researchers, ideation was ranked first for producing important results, so the thing scientists value most is the thing they hand over least.

The scenario modeling is what I keep returning to. With today's capabilities widely adopted, Anthropic estimates AI could take over a median 38% of research time. Even if every non-physical step were automated, fields heavy on lab work (biology, chemistry, materials) top out around 3-4x, because roughly 40% of their time is physical. Anthropic itself calls these "illustrative scenarios, not forecasts."

I would hold all of it loosely. This is Anthropic measuring its own product, and the survey respondents applied for Anthropic's AI-for-science credits, so they likely skew toward heavy AI users. My read, and I might be wrong: the near-term story in science is not machines having ideas, it is who owns the pipetting.

## 3. Anthropic: Claude helps a small team solve the first 3D structure of a bacterial "vault" particle
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-vault-particles-with-claude
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Hands-on / stakes-first with honest caveats: the useful detail is Claude's mistakes, not the headline. Format: LinkedIn. **Note: published Nov 4, so about 3 days old, outside the 48h bar; included to reach 3 because the other candidates were weaker or older.**

A team with no experience in huge protein assemblies went from a DNA sequence search to a first 3D structural model in 34 hours, with Claude doing much of the work. Anthropic's biology lab published the result on Nov 4, alongside a preprint.

The subject is the "vault," a hollow protein barrel found in many animal cells whose job is still unknown. Nobody had shown that bacteria or the viruses that infect them build one. Claude searched a database of about 15 billion predicted proteins, tried 44 candidates in a cell-free system, and the team went on to confirm four vault-like particles by cryo-EM (imaging frozen molecules with an electron microscope), two from phages and two from bacteria. The first solved structure has 96 copies of one protein versus 78 in mammals, and encloses about 25% more volume. A purification step that normally takes about 3 days took roughly 90 minutes after Claude proposed loading cell contents straight onto the column.

The part I trust most is that the post lists Claude's errors. A protocol it wrote included an additive that blocked DNA uptake, costing about two weeks, and it skipped a standard cryo-EM step early on, producing a map blended with its own mirror image.

Worth holding loosely: this is self-reported, the preprint was not yet live at publication, the vaults were only made in E. coli, and their function is still unknown. The cryo-EM work also used hundreds of GPUs. My read: the interesting claim is not "AI discovers biology," it is that a small team can now work outside its expertise if it checks the AI's work.
