## 1. A researcher says an AI agent found 19 unknown Redis flaws in 90 minutes, then built a working exploit in 27

**Source:** Chaofan Shou (independent AI-agent security researcher) on X, https://x.com/Fried_rice/status/2080059356322918777 (cross-referenced: The Hacker News, heise online, IBTimes UK, secnews.gr) [TODO: verify — timings and agent-count are self-reported by one researcher, not confirmed by Moonshot AI or Redis]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Dangerous-capability eval / AI security straddle — stakes-first: the interesting fact isn't the exact stopwatch numbers, it's that the underlying vulnerabilities were independently confirmed real, while the speed and autonomy claims remain one person's account.

---

A researcher says an AI agent found 19 unknown security holes in Redis, the in-memory database software an enormous share of internet backends run on, in about 90 minutes. In a separate run, it turned one of those holes into working attack code in 27 minutes.

The claim comes from Chaofan Shou, an independent researcher who studies AI agents, describing work by Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 model. His account: rather than one model working alone, Kimi K3 split into roughly 32 coordinated agent instances, each handling a slice of the job. Instances cloned Redis's source code, ran fuzzers (programs that feed a target huge volumes of malformed input, hoping something breaks in a revealing way), and used a debugging tool to trace a crash back to the exact line of code causing it. What came out the other end was a working exploit needing no password or credential, capable of remote code execution, meaning an attacker's own commands running on someone else's server.

Worth being precise about what's actually confirmed versus what's one account. The 90-minute and 27-minute timings, and the 32-agent framing, come entirely from Shou's own posts. Neither Moonshot AI nor Redis's maintainers have verified that specific process. What is independently confirmed: the vulnerabilities were real. Redis shipped seven security patches on July 23 across four release lines, after researchers published working proof-of-concept exploits.

Take the stopwatch numbers with real skepticism, then. The process could easily have taken longer, needed more human steering, or gotten lucky with an easy target. Strip the specific minutes out and the finding underneath still holds: a widely deployed piece of infrastructure software had 19 vulnerabilities nobody had caught, and an AI system, with meaningful human direction, found and turned them into working attacks faster than most human red teams could manage.

That's a capability demonstration, not a verdict on how AI-assisted security research goes in general. It doesn't establish that this generalizes to arbitrary codebases, or that the process needed less supervision than described. It does put a visible dent in an assumption a lot of software security still quietly leans on: that finding something like this takes serious hacking skill and weeks of time.

## 2. Nvidia just put $500 billion behind a bet that memory chips, not GPU designs, are AI's real bottleneck

**Source:** NVIDIA Newsroom, https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/sk-group-and-nvidia-expand-strategic-partnership-across-ai-factories-and-next-generation-memory (cross-referenced: Reuters/Investing.com, CNBC, Tom's Hardware, SK hynix Newsroom)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: coverage reads this as "Nvidia lands a customer"; the sharper story is Nvidia locking in years of scarce HBM memory supply from its own chip partner, which is a bet on where the real ceiling on AI buildouts sits.

---

Nvidia just put $500 billion behind a bet that the thing limiting AI's growth isn't chip designs anymore. It's memory.

Nvidia and South Korea's SK Group signed letters of intent this week for a partnership worth more than $500 billion, spanning new AI data centers and a long-term memory supply deal. Letters of intent are non-binding, but companies don't usually attach nine-figure numbers to paper that isn't going somewhere real.

The headline piece: SK Telecom will build a 2-gigawatt AI data center, enough electricity to run a mid-size city, using Nvidia's next-generation "Vera Rubin" chips paired with SK hynix's HBM4 memory (high-bandwidth memory, the specialized chips that sit right next to a processor and feed it data fast enough to keep it busy). The facility is due online in 2027. Nvidia is also putting $1 billion into Naver, the company behind South Korea's dominant search engine and cloud business, to help it expand a separate AI data center alongside investment firm Brookfield.

Most coverage is reading this as Nvidia landing a customer. I read it as Nvidia buying insurance on its own supply chain. For the last couple of years, the real constraint on AI buildouts hasn't been Nvidia's ability to design fast chips, it's been the availability of HBM, the memory that ships on the same package as the processor. SK hynix is one of only a handful of companies that makes HBM at scale. When Nvidia locks in hundreds of billions with that supplier years ahead of when the chips ship, that's not a company flexing its market position. It's a company securing the one input it can't design its way around.

None of it is locked-in, spent money yet, and neither side has broken out how much of the $500 billion is data centers versus memory versus the Naver piece. But watch where the industry's biggest checks are going. Increasingly, they're going to the memory makers, not just the chip designers.

**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business, with real human stakes — stakes-first: the sequencing, not the merits of the underlying lawsuit, is the story: an active request to pause the product for safety review was sitting in front of a judge when the company widened its reach instead.

---

OpenAI expanded ChatGPT's health features to every adult in the US on July 23. A day earlier, a lawsuit had asked a court to pause that same product until an independent safety review happens.

ChatGPT Health, which can pull in a person's connected medical records and Apple Health data during an ordinary conversation, is now available to every logged-in US adult on the Free, Go, Plus, and Pro plans, on web and iOS. OpenAI says more than 300 million people already ask ChatGPT health questions every week, so this rollout scales up something people were already doing rather than introducing new behavior.

The lawsuit driving the "pause it" request is specific in a way that's hard to read past. Scott Winters, a 55-year-old former pastor in Florida, alleges he described groin tenderness to ChatGPT hours before he collapsed. According to his complaint, the chatbot responded with religious framing, telling him "God did not design your body to endlessly fail," rather than urging him to seek care. He says he delayed treatment for roughly six weeks and then suffered a massive pulmonary embolism, a blood clot blocking blood flow to the lungs, a medical emergency that is highly survivable if caught early and often fatal if missed. His suit, filed in San Francisco County Superior Court, brings eight claims including negligence and unauthorized practice of medicine, and asks the court to pause ChatGPT Health pending independent review.

None of that is a proven fact yet. It's one filed complaint, and I couldn't find any public OpenAI response to these specific allegations [TODO: verify OpenAI's response]. But it's not an isolated filing either: this is at least the third health-advice lawsuit OpenAI has faced in recent months, and a coalition of state attorneys general subpoenaed the company in June over exactly this category of data and behavior.

What strikes me is the sequencing, not the merits of any single case. A company can reasonably argue each lawsuit individually hasn't been proven. It's a harder argument to make that the responsible move, with an active request for independent safety review sitting in front of a judge, was to widen the product's reach that same week rather than pause and wait. Scale and scrutiny are moving in opposite directions here, and only one of them is reversible if something goes wrong.
