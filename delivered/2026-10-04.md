## 1. An AI model helped find a critical file-server bug; an attacker was hitting it within a day
**Source:** https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/03/anthropics-super-bug-hunting-model-mythos-is-hardcore-good-at-math-as-latest-vuln-under-attack-shows/5300933 (secondary; seen via search snippet only, egress blocked) [TODO: verify against Horizon3 / VulnCheck write-ups]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter (security frame)
**Frame / angle:** AI security (not model misbehavior). Stakes-first: the disclosure-to-exploitation gap is now hours, and AI-assisted bug finding is producing real CVEs.

An AI model helped a researcher find a critical hole in a widely used file server. Within a day of public disclosure, an attacker in China was already hitting vulnerable machines in the US and Japan.

Per The Register (Oct 3), Horizon3's Zach Hanley used Anthropic's Mythos model to find CVE-2026-61500 in Rejetto HTTP File Server, rated 9.8 out of 10 for severity. VulnCheck's decoy servers picked up the attacker. [TODO: verify]

The bug itself is almost embarrassingly human. The server builds the secret that protects admin logins from a random-number generator never meant for secrets, and it also leaks some of that generator's outputs at login. Collect a handful of login responses, work out the generator's internal state, and you can forge an administrator session. It is the kind of math-flavored mistake that is tedious for people to spot and, apparently, easier for a model.

I'm filing this as a security story, not a safety one: no model misbehaved. A defender used a capable tool as intended, and the speed gap showed up anyway, because the fix (version 3.2.1) has to reach every server owner before attackers do. I can't tell from public reporting whether the attacker used AI at all. Public bugs get hit quickly regardless.

What I can say is narrower. Finding bugs with these models is real enough that the people doing it are getting critical CVEs, and the window between disclosure and exploitation is measured in hours. If you run any self-hosted software, updating promptly just got more valuable.

## 2. White House answers the AI labs' call to slow down with a "Super Intelligence Force"
**Source:** https://www.washingtonpost.com/politics/2026/10/04/trump-launches-super-intelligence-force-after-calls-ai-slowdown/ (secondary; search snippets only, egress blocked) [TODO: verify against White House statement / full reporting]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter (governance frame)
**Frame / angle:** Governance / policy. Stakes-first: the labs asked for brakes, the government answered with a 120-day study, and the membership tells you what it's built to do.

After Anthropic and OpenAI publicly said the industry shouldn't keep scaling at maximum speed, the White House answered on Oct 4 with a new body called the "Super Intelligence Force." Its first job is a 120-day study, not a slowdown.

Per reporting from CNBC and the Washington Post (I only saw excerpts, not the full pieces), Trump named four officials to run it: DNI Jay Clayton as chair, FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson, the Pentagon's Emil Michael and OPM Director Scott Kupor. It reports to him and chief of staff Susie Wiles, and is meant to coordinate with consumers, public-interest groups, religious organizations, critical-infrastructure providers and the AI companies. Earlier, Trump rejected the slowdown call, saying the government would not "hinder or stifle" the industry's growth. [TODO: verify quotes and dates against primary sources]

Reasonable people disagree here. The administration's worry that slowing down hands an edge to Chinese labs is a real argument, and the labs' calls haven't fully answered it. A study group is also a cheap, reversible step compared with binding rules.

My read of the membership: the chair runs intelligence, one member sits at the competition regulator, one at the Pentagon, one at the federal hiring office. Nobody on the announced list is a technical safety or evaluation specialist, at least as reported. ## 3. Meta: six math papers written with a plain chat window, five answering open problems
**Source:** https://research.meta.ai/blog/solving-open-research-problems-together (primary, but egress blocked; details from secondary summaries) [TODO: verify against Meta's post]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter (LinkedIn)
**Frame / angle:** Capability demo / skeptical-but-interested. Stakes-first: research-grade AI help in a consumer chat product, with the credit caveat Meta itself flags.

Meta says its mathematicians used the regular meta.ai chat window, with no custom research setup, to help write six papers, five of which answer open problems. One had been open since 2015.

Meta's Oct 2 research post, as I read it through summaries, says the team used the Thinking Mode of Muse Spark 1.1 and 1.2. Results include a group-theory counterexample (group theory is a branch of algebra), a disproof of a conjecture about "evolution algebras," and a proof that solutions to a particular wave-type equation blow up in finite time. [TODO: verify specifics]

Credit is where I'd slow down. Meta itself notes that other teams reported answers to some of the same problems independently: an August preprint on the random-points result, a Sept 16 counterexample from an AI agent called Nilradical, and separate work on the evolution-algebra question. My read is that this looks less like a lone breakthrough and more like several groups and tools reaching the same open questions at about the same time.

Skeptically, "open problem" covers a huge range, from famous to obscure corners few people had tried, and I can't judge the difficulty of these. The claim I find more interesting is about the interface. Mathematicians still had to choose the direction and check every step, and they did it in a consumer chat product. If that holds up, the barrier to AI-assisted research is closer to knowing which question to ask than to having special infrastructure.
