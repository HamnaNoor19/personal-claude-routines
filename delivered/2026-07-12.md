## 1. A Fields Medalist let an AI agent loose on 27-year-old code he wrote himself — and it found bugs he'd missed

**Source:** https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/07/11/old-and-new-apps-via-modern-coding-agents/ (cross-referenced: Hacker News, Tech Times)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Hands-on observation (secondhand, high-credibility source) — stakes-first: this is a trust-calibration signal from an unusually rigorous reviewer, not another capability benchmark.

---

Terence Tao, widely considered one of the best living mathematicians, spent this week letting an AI coding agent loose on code he wrote in 1999. He published the full transcripts, bugs and all.

Back in 1999 he'd written about two dozen Java applets for teaching, visualizations of honeycombs, Besicovitch sets, that kind of thing. Java 1.0 stopped working in modern browsers years ago and the applets went dark. He asked Claude Code, Anthropic's coding agent, to port them to JavaScript, and it finished the whole batch in a matter of hours. Along the way it introduced exactly one minor bug of its own and caught two bugs in Tao's original 27-year-old code that he'd never noticed.

That result was clean enough that he kept going. A special-relativity visualizer he'd started in 1999 and abandoned because the code got too complicated finally got finished, in a couple hours of what he calls "guided interaction." Then he built a new interactive explorer for the Gilbreath conjecture, timed to accompany an actual paper on the topic he co-authored, and posted that conversation transcript too.

The interesting part isn't that an agent can port legacy code. It's who is doing the trusting and how carefully he's scoping it. Tao is explicit that this is fine for supplementary visualizations, not for anything load-bearing in an actual proof. That boundary, drawn by someone this exacting, tells you more about where agentic coding is actually reliable right now than another benchmark number would.

## 2. SK Hynix just had the second-biggest US stock listing ever — and it isn't even a chip designer, it's a memory maker

**Source:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/10/sk-hynix-skhy-stock-nasdaq.html (cross-referenced: TechCrunch, Fortune, Bloomberg)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — stakes-first: the actual bottleneck on AI progress right now may be memory supply, not model architecture.

---

The biggest constraint on how fast AI labs can actually deploy new models right now might not be compute chips, it's the memory sitting next to them, and investors just made a very loud bet on who controls that.

SK Hynix listed on Nasdaq on Friday, and by the time the market closed its stock was up 13%, at $168.01, after pricing at $149. The company raised $26.5 billion, the second-largest US stock listing ever, trailing only SpaceX's IPO four weeks earlier. The US portion of the offering was oversubscribed roughly 7 times.

What SK Hynix actually makes is HBM, high-bandwidth memory, the specialized chips that sit directly next to AI accelerators like Nvidia's GPUs and feed them data fast enough to keep them busy. The company controls something like 60% of that market by revenue, and it's been one of the tightest supply constraints in AI datacenter buildouts for over a year now. The chairman's quote to CNBC was blunt: "demand is enormous."

That money isn't just sitting there either. It's funding a $4 billion advanced-packaging plant in Indiana and a $390 billion cluster of new fabs in Yongin, South Korea, both aimed at the same thing: making more HBM, faster.

I'd be careful reading this as proof AI memory demand is now permanent rather than cyclical. Chip markets have overbuilt before and eaten the correction later. But a 7x-oversubscribed, second-largest-ever US listing is investors saying, with real money, that they don't think this round of scarcity is temporary. Worth knowing that the thing gating "how fast can AI get better" this year might be a memory fab in Korea as much as anything happening in a training run.

## 3. Anthropic's most expensive model ever is a $10/$50-per-million-token price signal, not a pricing-page footnote

**Source:** https://www.androidauthority.com/claude-fable-5-free-extension-3685103/ (cross-referenced: Forbes, Digital Applied, FullStackEvolved, Anthropic's own SpaceX-deal post)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: this reads as a routine pricing update, but two backlash-driven delays plus a record compute deal make it a real signal about frontier-model compute costs.

---

Anthropic just pushed back the deadline on its own flagship model for the second time in a month, and the reason matters more than the deadline itself.

Claude Fable 5, Anthropic's newest and most capable model, launched free on paid Claude plans on June 9. The company originally said free access would end June 22. Then it extended to July 8. After more user pushback, it extended again to tonight, July 12, letting subscribers use Fable 5 for up to half their weekly usage at no extra cost in the meantime. After 11:59pm Pacific tonight, using it requires prepaid credits billed at $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens, exactly double the rate for Claude Opus 4.8 and the most expensive pricing Anthropic has ever put on a model anyone can actually buy.

What makes this worth noting isn't the price tag by itself. It's the timing. In May, Anthropic signed a deal for full access to SpaceX's Colossus 1 data center, more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs (the chips AI models are trained and run on) and over 300 megawatts of power, one of the largest compute acquisitions any AI lab has made public. That deal was supposed to ease exactly this kind of squeeze. Two months later, Anthropic is still delaying, still walking back a subscription promise under user pressure, and still charging double for its top model once the free tier runs out.

I don't think this means Anthropic is in trouble. I think it means running a frontier-class model at scale still costs more than even a company with hundreds of thousands of extra GPUs can comfortably absorb into a flat subscription price. The $10/$50 rate isn't a marketing number, it's closer to what the compute for that class of model genuinely costs right now, and that's a more honest data point than most of what gets published about model economics.
