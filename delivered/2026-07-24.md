## 1. Claude Opus 5 launches at half Fable 5's price — and beats it on more than half the benchmarks that matter

**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5 (cross-referenced: CNBC, VentureBeat, Fortune, Bloomberg, officechai, the-decoder)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation — frame-shift: Anthropic's own framing is "budget model, nearly as good as flagship"; its own benchmark table shows Opus 5 outright winning most of the head-to-head comparisons, which is a sharper story than "nearly as good."

---

Anthropic's launch messaging for Claude Opus 5 frames it as the budget option: nearly as capable as the flagship Fable 5 model, at half the price. The benchmark table in the same announcement tells a slightly sharper story than that framing lets on.

Opus 5 shipped July 24 at $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens, half of Fable 5's $10/$50. On Anthropic's own comparison, drawn from nine benchmarks the two models share, Opus 5 doesn't just come close to Fable 5, it wins five of them outright: agentic terminal coding, search, computer use, and business workflow tasks. On ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark built to test genuinely novel problem-solving rather than pattern-matched skills, Opus 5 scored 30.2%. On CursorBench 3.2, it landed within half a percentage point of Fable 5's best score at max reasoning effort, for roughly half the per-task cost, and it beat Fable 5's top OSWorld 2.0 result at about a third of the cost.

Fable 5 isn't obsolete. It still edges Opus 5 on DeepSWE v1.1 (69.7% versus 68.8%) and a held-out legal-reasoning benchmark, and Fable ships with extra safety classifiers that route sensitive cyber and bio queries away from it, something Opus 5 doesn't have layered on top the same way.

Worth being honest about the "half the price" framing too. Some of the per-task cost comparisons come from Anthropic's own benchmark runs, where Opus 5 finished the same tasks using fewer tokens or turns. That's a real efficiency story, but it isn't the same claim as "same tokens, half the price."

What I keep coming back to: if the cheaper model wins the majority of your own head-to-head benchmarks, "flagship" is starting to describe something other than raw capability.

## 2. A security firm found a single message could break Claude Cowork out of its sandbox and onto your whole Mac

**Source:** https://www.accomplish.ai/blog/sharedroot-escaping-claude-cowork-sandbox/ (cross-referenced: The Hacker News, CyberSecurityNews, GBHackers, IT-Connect, News4Hackers) [TODO: verify exact affected-user count — the "~500,000 macOS users" figure appears consistently across secondary coverage but traces back to a single original disclosure this session couldn't fetch directly]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security — frame-shift: most headlines read this as "AI agent escaped its sandbox," which sounds agentic; the sharper story is a fairly ordinary container-security mistake (an over-broad host mount) chained to an unrelated Linux kernel bug, plus a vendor choosing to change a default instead of fixing the hole.

---

Claude's Cowork feature lets an AI agent work directly on files on your own computer, not a sealed cloud sandbox. A security firm called Accomplish AI just showed that, running locally on a Mac, one instruction to that agent could hand it read and write access to your entire machine, SSH keys and cloud credentials included.

Cowork's local mode runs the agent inside a Linux virtual machine, or VM (a walled-off mini-computer meant to contain the agent's actions to files you've explicitly shared). Accomplish found that Cowork actually mounts your whole host filesystem into that VM, read-write, reachable by anything that gets root access inside it. They chained that design to a real, separately disclosed Linux kernel bug (CVE-2026-46331, nicknamed "pedit COW") to get root inside the VM, and the wide-open mount did the rest. Their demo: connect one folder, send one short message, watch the agent read and write files far outside that folder, no permission prompt at any point.

Roughly 500,000 macOS users running Cowork locally were exposed. The part I'd flag over the bug itself is Anthropic's response: they closed the report as "informative," their label for real-but-not-fixed, instead of patching the sharing design. What actually cut the exposure is that Cowork's newest version now defaults to cloud execution, which sidesteps the flaw by removing the shared host mount entirely. Anyone still opting into local execution is reportedly still exposed.

Narrowly, this shows a sandbox sharing "the whole host, read-write" with anything that reaches root inside it is one kernel bug away from not being a sandbox. It doesn't show anything about the model choosing to do this. The agent followed an instruction, and the instruction happened to be a working exploit chain someone handed it.

## 3. Stripe is reportedly paying close to $10B for the company that decides which AI model your app talks to

**Source:** https://www.theinformation.com/briefings/stripe-talks-buy-startup-openrouter (cross-referenced: WSJ via Yahoo Finance, Benzinga, PYMNTS, BigGo Finance)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: coverage is framing this as "Stripe getting into AI"; the sharper point is consolidation of the model-routing layer, paired with Stripe's earlier Metronome acquisition (usage billing), as two ends of the same pipe.

---

Stripe is reportedly in talks to buy OpenRouter for close to $10 billion. The price is the headline, but the more interesting part is what OpenRouter actually controls: the layer that decides which AI model your app talks to when you haven't hardcoded a choice yourself.

OpenRouter lets developers call hundreds of different models, Claude, GPT, Gemini, open-weight options, all of them, through one shared API instead of wiring an app to a single provider. The Information first reported the acquisition talks, and WSJ and other outlets have since corroborated them. OpenRouter raised at a $1.3 billion valuation this past May, so a $10 billion price would be roughly 7.7 times that two months later, and about 200 times the company's own reported annualized revenue of $50 million.

Most coverage is framing this as "Stripe getting into AI." I think that undersells the acquisition. Whoever owns the routing layer has real leverage over which model gets prioritized by default, plus visibility into usage patterns across a huge number of AI apps that never built their own routing logic.

It also isn't Stripe's first move in this direction. The company already owns Metronome, a usage-based billing platform a lot of AI companies use to charge by the token or the API call. Metronome answers "how do we get paid." OpenRouter would answer "which model gets used." Controlling both ends of that pipe is a different kind of position than owning either piece alone.

None of this is settled. WSJ's reporting notes the talks could still fall apart, and OpenRouter has reportedly held early conversations with Databricks as well. But the price being discussed says something about how much a model-routing layer is worth to a payments company right now.
