## 1. DeepMind's Nobel-winning AlphaFold team no longer exists as a unit — and three of its top researchers just left for Anthropic

**Source:** https://www.engadget.com/2225849/google-shuts-down-alphafold/ (originating from Financial Times reporting)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: coverage is reading "AlphaFold team disbanded" as the science ending; the sharper story is where the senior talent actually went and what that says about DeepMind's shifting priorities.

---

Google DeepMind's AlphaFold team, the one that won a Nobel Prize in 2024 for predicting how proteins fold, has effectively stopped existing as a dedicated group. Most of the researchers who wrote the original AlphaFold papers have been reassigned over the past year, according to reporting picked up across half a dozen outlets this week.

The framing you'll see is "DeepMind shuts down AlphaFold." That's not quite it. The science is still happening, mostly under Isomorphic Labs, DeepMind's drug-discovery spinoff, working on enzyme design and genomics. What actually ended is the team as a standing unit built around one problem.

The sharper detail is where three of its most senior people went. John Jumper, who shared that Nobel with DeepMind's Demis Hassabis, left for Anthropic in June. Two other core AlphaFold authors, Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel, followed him there within weeks. Almost a quarter of the full-time DeepMind researchers who authored the original AlphaFold papers are no longer at the company at all. DeepMind says the remaining people moved onto Gemini-focused work, plus fusion and genomics projects, part of a stated shift toward general AI systems meant to assist scientists rather than single-purpose models built for one grand challenge.

I read this less as "AlphaFold is over" and more as a data point on how frontier labs are currently valuing prestige science teams against general-purpose agent work, and on how tight the market for senior AI-for-science researchers has gotten between DeepMind and Anthropic specifically. A Nobel Prize apparently wasn't enough to keep the team's shape once the parent lab's priorities moved elsewhere.

## 2. A new paper ran the actual test on "can AI automate AI research" — and the answer is more specific than either side wants

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27191
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability eval — stakes-first: ties directly to the live AI-pacing debate by supplying an actual empirical test of whether agents can do open-ended AI research, not just execute known steps.

---

There's a specific empirical question underneath a lot of the "AI is about to automate AI research" talk, and a new paper just ran the actual test.

The people asking whether frontier AI companies need government help pacing themselves (over 1,100 of them signed a letter days ago making exactly that ask) are worried about a fairly specific trigger: AI systems getting good enough to design and run their own research faster than humans can check the results. Whether that's close depends on whether AI agents can actually do open-ended research, not just execute known steps.

A new paper from Peter Kirgis, Sayash Kapoor, and roughly two dozen co-authors tries to measure that directly. Their method, which they call a "shadow evaluation," is clever: take an unpublished, high-quality paper still under review at NeurIPS 2026 (one of the top AI research conferences), hand a frontier AI agent the same open research question the human authors were working on, and let those same authors grade what the agent produces. That sidesteps the two weak options researchers have been stuck with: narrow benchmark tasks that don't capture open-endedness, and dumping AI-written papers into an already overloaded peer review system.

They ran this on two submissions, giving agents six days and thousands of dollars of compute each. The agents finished all the engineering, writing and running the code, with no human help. On the actual research question, in both cases, they didn't get anywhere close.

The part that matters more than the headline number is what it separates. That's a narrower and more useful finding than either "AI can't do real research" or "AI research automation is imminent." The execution part looks solved. The part that's still missing is the judgment call about what question to chase and how to interpret what comes back, which happens to be the part that actually determines whether a result means anything. Two case studies is a small sample to hang a trend on, and the authors call this "early evidence" for a reason, but it's a more concrete data point than most of what's driving the automation-timeline debate right now.

## 3. Microsoft says its new cyber model beat Anthropic's flagship benchmark score — the fine print tells a different story

**Source:** https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/07/28/microsoft-ai-releases-mai-cyber-1-flash-a-5b-active-parameter-cyber-model-that-pushes-mdash-to-95-95-on-cybergym/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation — skeptical pushback on hype: the "beats Mythos" framing undersells that this is a self-reported, system-level score with a fallback model doing part of the work.

---

Microsoft says its new cybersecurity model just beat Anthropic's flagship on a benchmark, and most coverage is running with the "beats Mythos" framing. The number underneath it is more interesting than that framing, and less clean.

CyberGym is a benchmark of 1,507 real vulnerability-reproduction tasks pulled from 188 open-source projects, essentially testing whether an AI can actually find and demonstrate a known security bug from scratch. Microsoft's own security harness, called MDASH, scored 95.95% on it this week using a new model built specifically for this, MAI-Cyber-1-Flash. That's roughly 12 points above the 83.8% Anthropic has reported for Claude Mythos on the same benchmark.

The part that matters more than the headline number is what's actually being measured. MAI-Cyber-1-Flash is a mixture-of-experts model (137 billion parameters total, but only 5 billion "active" on any given query, which is why it's cheap to run), and it isn't working alone. MDASH routes about 90% of tasks to it and hands the hardest 10% off to GPT-5.4. So 95.95% is a score for that whole routed system, cheaper model plus fallback plus Microsoft's own harness logic, not a clean head-to-head between one model and Mythos. Before this update, the same harness using only off-the-shelf models scored 88.4%, so the defensible claim is closer to "a purpose-built small model plus smart routing improved our system by about 7.5 points at roughly half the cost." That's a solid result on its own, and it didn't need the beat-Mythos framing to be one.

It's also self-reported. The score hasn't shown up on CyberGym's public leaderboard yet, and nobody outside Microsoft has independently reproduced it. Anthropic's number has the same limitation, for what it's worth; self-reported benchmarks are the norm right now, not the exception. Worth remembering the next time a benchmark chart gets read as "Model A beats Model B."
