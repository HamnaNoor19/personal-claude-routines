## 1. Google DeepMind's Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash treats video generation like an iterative dev workflow

**Source:** https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/build-with-gemini-omni-1-1-flash/ (Google official blog, published ~Aug 27, 2026)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Product launch / dev tooling. Frames not taken: model release / capability evaluation (would over-focus on the Elo ranking), hands-on observation (haven't used the API). Angle: frame-shift — most coverage reads this as "AI video keeps getting better"; the sharper story is the 10x draft-vs-final pricing split, which is a different product shape than one-shot video generation.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: product launch / dev tooling. Angle: frame-shift.

---

Most coverage of Google's new video model is going to read as "AI video keeps getting better," which is true but not the interesting part.

Gemini Omni 1.1 Flash, released this week by Google DeepMind, extends generated video scenes from 10 seconds to a continuous 40 seconds. It does this by having the model read up to 10 seconds of prior video as context when extending a scene, instead of just the last frame, which is what caused the visible jump cuts in earlier scene-extension tools. It's currently ranked #1 on Video Arena, a crowdsourced leaderboard where people vote on video pairs head to head, with an Elo score (a ranking system borrowed from chess) of 1,515.

The part I keep coming back to is the pricing structure, not the leaderboard ranking. Google shipped two tiers: a 360p draft mode that renders about 60% faster and costs a third of standard resolution, at $0.03 a second, and a 4K final render at $0.30 a second, a 10x jump. You can also pin the first and last frame of a clip to lock in camera movement, and pass earlier clips back in as references to keep a character looking consistent across shots.

That pricing split changes what building on this actually looks like: iterate cheaply in low-res, then pay ten times as much once you commit to a final render. That's closer to writing throwaway drafts before a final compile, or previewing a rough render before spending GPU time on the final pass in 3D software, than it is to the one-shot, expensive calls most video generation APIs have shipped as. It's aimed pretty clearly at developers building pipelines on the API, not just people typing prompts into a consumer app.

I haven't touched the API myself, so I can't tell you how the 360p drafts actually hold up next to the final render, or whether the context-aware extension stays coherent past a couple of cuts. An Elo score is a popularity contest among human raters on specific prompts, not a guarantee it'll hold up on your use case. But the draft-then-commit pricing is worth watching — if it works as advertised, it's a pattern the rest of the video-gen field will probably copy.

---

OpenAI is testing a coding agent that doesn't stop when you close the tab, decides on its own what to work on next, and will message you first instead of waiting to be asked. Nobody at OpenAI announced this. WIRED reporters found it described in code sitting inside the product itself.

The feature is called Persistent Mode, built for Codex, OpenAI's coding agent. Today, a Codex session runs a task and then shuts down after minutes or hours. The code describes something built to keep running until you explicitly tell it to stop, plus a "proactivity" feature: the agent generates its own follow-up tasks, keeps its state across sessions instead of resetting, and can reach out to you unprompted. OpenAI confirmed to WIRED that it's testing this, but said there are no immediate plans to ship it.

Worth being precise about what this is and isn't. There's no system card (the documentation labs typically publish alongside an actual launch), no benchmark, no safety documentation, nothing OpenAI has published about how this behaves in practice. What exists is code a reporter found and a confirmation that testing is underway. The specific behaviors described could change completely, or never leave the lab.

Even so, the shape of the idea is worth sitting with. A tool you invoke and a tool that decides on its own what to do next, then interrupts you about it, are different things to manage. The first one waits for you. The second one expects you to react to it. Sam Altman has said more than once that he wants ChatGPT to work like a standing personal assistant rather than something you open and close, and this fits that pattern directly. If something like this ships, using a coding agent stops being "I ask, it answers" and starts looking more like managing a fast, literal junior engineer who doesn't wait to be asked what's next.

I don't think that's necessarily bad. I do think it's a different job than the one most people signed up for when they started using a coding assistant.

## 3. A new paper says the safety rules in your coding agent can quietly stop working the longer a session runs

**Source:** arXiv:2608.22752, "The Compaction Cliff in Long-Running AI Agent Memory," Zerhoudi, Mitrovic & Granitzer, accepted to CIKM 2026 [TODO: verify — direct arXiv fetch was blocked this session; figures and framing are drawn from cross-referenced secondary summaries, not a direct read of the paper]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security (straddles alignment/control — see note below). Frames not taken: interpretability, capability evals, governance/policy. Angle: stakes-first — leads with what changes for a reader who uses these tools, before naming the paper.

Frame: AI security. Angle: stakes-first. *(Straddle note: this also brushes against alignment/control — whether a stated behavioral constraint stays in force over time — but the paper's actual contribution is a failure mode plus a fix, not a claim about model intent, so security is the sharper frame here.)*

---

A new paper found that the safety rule you give a coding agent can quietly stop working partway through a long session, not from an attack, but from how the agent manages its own memory.

Long-running agents, ones that keep working across many steps rather than answering once and stopping, can't hold a whole session in memory forever. Most periodically compress the conversation history into a shorter summary to make room, a process called context compaction. The paper looks at what happens when a safety instruction, something like "always ask before sending an email," sits in that history next to routine task logs, and both get compressed at the same rate. Task logs can lose detail and stay useful. A safety rule usually needs close to its exact wording to keep working as a hard constraint, and a paraphrased, shortened version of it may not reliably block what it was meant to block.

The authors, Saber Zerhoudi, Jelena Mitrovic, and Michael Granitzer, tested this across 20 production agent configurations, in work accepted to CIKM 2026, a peer-reviewed data and knowledge management conference. Their sharpest number: on Claude Code, Anthropic's coding agent, running its built-in "/compact" memory-compression feature on the Sonnet 4.6 model, 53% of safety rules survived one round of compaction, falling to 10% after five rounds. They call it the Compaction Cliff. [TODO: verify exact figures and quotes against the primary source, arXiv:2608.22752 — this session could not fetch arxiv.org directly and relied on cross-referenced secondary summaries.]

I'm reading this mainly as a security finding, not an alignment one: if extending a session on purpose forces repeated compaction, the same mechanism doubles as a way to wear down a constraint without touching it directly. That fix, Knowledge Triage, sorts an agent's stored knowledge by type so a safety rule and a log entry get separate retention treatment instead of being flattened into one summary together.

Worth being precise about what this isn't. Nobody is reporting this was exploited against a live system, and the 53-to-10 numbers are specific to this study's Claude Code and Sonnet 4.6 setup, not a universal constant. Compaction as a design choice is common across coding agents generally, so the gap plausibly isn't limited to the one tool measured here. What stood out to me is that they tested something a lot of us actually run, which lands differently than a lab-only setup would.
