## 1. OpenAI is letting four outside safety groups watch its models while they're still being trained
**Source:** https://openai.com/index/our-first-phase-of-embedded-assessments/ (OpenAI blog, "Our first phase of embedded assessments," Nov 16, 2026; not fetched directly, details via search excerpts and coverage such as Quartz https://qz.com/openai-four-independent-safety-evaluators-embedded-assessments-111626 and Benzinga https://www.benzinga.com/markets/private-markets/26/11/63307780/openai-brings-four-outside-research-groups-into-ai-safety-testing) [TODO: verify against the post]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: evals (outside evaluation access), not alignment research or interpretability, though Goodfire's interpretability role is mentioned. Angle: stakes-first; this changes who gets to look and when, and its credibility rests on pay and publication terms, not the announcement. Format: LinkedIn.

The outside groups that safety-test AI models have mostly been handed a nearly finished product shortly before launch. OpenAI just let four of them in while its models are still being trained. That changes who gets to look, and when.

Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire are the four. As reported, METR will watch what OpenAI calls its largest reinforcement learning run to date (the training stage where a model learns by trial and reward), with access to saved snapshots of the model, transcripts of its practice attempts and OpenAI's own monitoring records. Apollo is placing six evaluators inside for four months to test whether models work around safety safeguards, including ones meant to keep future models in check. Transluce will check how good OpenAI's own safety tests are, and Goodfire brings interpretability researchers (people who study a model's inner workings, not just its answers). I keep comparing it to a financial audit, which is only as credible as its independence and what gets published. The independence terms look careful: per coverage, Apollo and Goodfire are paid in ways that don't depend on what they find, and METR and Transluce take no direct payment, though they may accept computing credits. Publication is murkier. One report says OpenAI's board-level safety committee sees findings unredacted, while OpenAI may redact confidential details from public versions. ## 2. OpenAI's free child-abuse image detector: same catch, 48% fewer images for human reviewers to look at
**Source:** https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/16/openai-releases-a-new-image-classifier-and-chatgpt-warnings-to-combat-csam/ (TechCrunch coverage of OpenAI's Nov 16, 2026 release and the "OpenAI CSAM Image Classifier" model card; neither fetched directly, details via search excerpts and coverage such as Fox News https://www.foxnews.com/tech/openai-child-safety-tools-target-ai-generated-abuse) [TODO: verify against OpenAI's model card]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security in the misuse sense (people using and spreading abusive imagery), not alignment or model behavior; the straddle is that one score detects AI-generated images. Angle: frame-shift; the headline number is about reviewer workload, not detection. Format: LinkedIn.

Every image a child-safety detector flags has to be checked by a person, and that person has to look at it. So the number I'd lead with from OpenAI's new detector is a reduction: 48% fewer images sent to human reviewers, while catching about the same amount of abuse.

OpenAI released the classifier on Nov 16, built with the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children and offered free through its API to vetted organizations, with access shared through the Tech Coalition and ROOST (industry child-safety and open-source trust-and-safety groups). It gives two scores, one for likely abuse material and one for whether an image looks AI-generated. In early partner testing, OpenAI reports, flagged images were about twice as likely to actually be abusive as with existing systems. [TODO: verify both figures against the model card]

I think that's the right thing to optimize. The people doing this review work have one of the most psychologically brutal jobs in tech, and every false alarm costs them something.

Two caveats worth keeping. These are OpenAI's own numbers from early testing, not an independent evaluation. And the model card says neither score is a legal finding or proof of where an image came from, which matters most for the "AI-generated" score, since telling real from synthetic is exactly where detectors tend to struggle.

ChatGPT also changed: people who try to generate or seek this material now get a warning and a 24-hour suspension, and repeat offenses can reportedly cost them the account. [TODO: verify suspension rules]

My guess, and the release doesn't show this, is that free access for smaller platforms will matter more than the ChatGPT changes, because most of this material circulates far from ChatGPT.

## 3. 20 of the 23 market insiders the Fed surveyed now name AI as a top risk to the financial system
**Source:** Federal Reserve, Financial Stability Report, Nov 13, 2026, https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/financial-stability-report.htm (not fetched directly, details via coverage such as Bloomberg https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-11-13/fed-sees-risks-tied-to-elevated-asset-prices-partly-driven-by-ai, Axios https://www.axios.com/2026/11/16/fed-ai-boom-financial-risks and TechTimes https://www.techtimes.com/articles/330781/20261116/fed-financial-stability-report-ai-data-center-debt-hits-31-investment-grade-issuance.htm) [TODO: verify figures against the report PDF]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business (not discourse or explainer). Angle: frame-shift; coverage leads with the 87% survey number, but the concrete finding is the web of debt linking big banks to data-center builders. Format: LinkedIn.

Twenty of the 23 market professionals the Federal Reserve surveyed this fall named AI as a top risk to the US financial system. In the spring it was about half. If you have a bank account or a retirement fund, the AI build-out is now part of your financial picture whether you use chatbots or not.

That 87% figure comes from the Fed's Financial Stability Report (Nov 13), and it leads most coverage. I'd hold it a bit loosely. It's a survey of 23 contacts, not the Fed's own forecast, and "AI" can stand for anything from a stock bubble to AI-powered cyberattacks. The next most cited worries were a broad drop in asset prices (52%) and higher long-term interest rates (48%). [TODO: verify]

The part I found more concrete is the plumbing. Per coverage, the largest US banks have close to $1 trillion of exposure to big tech firms, data-center developers, companies set up specifically to finance computing hardware, private lenders and utilities, and the report says the links among them "create vulnerabilities that necessitate close monitoring." Default rates in private credit (loans made by investment funds rather than banks, a big source of data-center money) have climbed since May to the upper end of their historical range. How much AI-related debt exists depends on who's counting: outlets cite figures from $680 billion to more than $1 trillion, and around $3 trillion once leases and guarantees are included. [TODO: verify which figure the Fed uses for what]

What the report shows is regulators tracing how AI money runs through banks. It doesn't show anything breaking. My read, and it's speculation: the scenario worth watching is a data-center tenant missing payments and the losses turning up somewhere nobody expected, more than AI stocks simply falling.
