## 1. Google opened its paper-error checker to everyone. The "35%" in the headlines is a survey answer, not an error rate.
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28277 (preprint, "Towards Automating Scientific Review with Google's Paper Assistant Tool"); public launch of Deep Review covered Oct 15, 2026 by https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20261015-google-deep-review/ and https://www.rdworldonline.com/google-launches-deep-review-ai-tool-to-help-researchers-catch-manuscript-errors/ [TODO: verify against Google's own launch post; primary site fetches failed from this environment, figures come from search summaries]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: product launch plus a correction of how it is being reported. Angle: stakes-first, with frame-shift on the 35% figure. Frames not taken: AI-replaces-peer-review take, deskilling think-piece.

If you have ever trusted a scientific result because it was "peer reviewed," this is worth a minute: Google has made its AI paper checker free to the public, and the number going around about it is being misreported.

The tool is called Deep Review (built from what Google calls its Paper Assistant Tool). Before launch it read more than 16,000 manuscripts at four computer science conferences, giving authors a private critique before submission. Headlines say it "caught errors in 35% of machine learning papers." From what I can find, that is not what was measured. In the ICML 2026 pilot (a major machine learning conference), 35.4% of authors who answered a survey said it pointed out significant problems. That is how often people said it helped, not how often papers were wrong.

The cleaner evidence is a benchmark in Google's preprint: on 29 known mathematical errors across 26 papers, the tool found 89.7%, against 55.2% for a single pass by Gemini 3.1 Pro. That is a small set, built from papers that were later corrected or retracted, and it is Google grading its own tool. At STOC (a theory conference), about one in ten authors said it found errors that would take over an hour to fix. Pilot users also reported cases where it wrongly challenged a correct proof.

My read is that the interesting part is not the percentage. It is that a checker which spends real compute re-deriving someone's argument is now something a grad student can run twice a week for free. I'm skeptical that this helps if reviewers start leaning on it; the authors themselves flag deskilling as a risk.

## 2. Microsoft paper: the "cheap" model costs 3.2x less per task, not 5x, because it needs far more tries
**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19292 ("Whose turn is it? Context-guided model routing for stateful agents", submitted Oct 13, 2026; discussed Oct 16) [TODO: verify numbers against the full paper; read via search summaries only]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: paper / technique. Angle: stakes-first for anyone budgeting an AI agent; price list vs real cost. Note: item is ~4 days from submission, borderline against the recency bar; chosen for lay-legible stakes. Frames not taken: model-comparison horse race.

If your team is choosing an AI model for an agent by comparing price lists, a new Microsoft-affiliated paper suggests the price list can mislead you by a wide margin.

The setup: an AI agent works through a multi-turn session, like a long back-and-forth fixing a spreadsheet. A "router" decides, turn by turn, whether to send the next step to a cheap model or an expensive one. The authors test this on spreadsheet tasks (SheetCopilot and SpreadsheetBench 2).

Their cost example is the part I'd remember. Claude Haiku 4.5 is priced at one fifth of GPT-5.6 Sol per input token. In their runs, though, Haiku made about 41 model calls and read 1.6 million input tokens per conversation, against roughly 25 calls and 0.9 million for the other models. Because it takes more steps and rereads more context, it ended up only 3.2x cheaper per task ($0.37 vs $1.18), while passing 16.4% of tasks against 27.1%. A mid-priced model, GPT-5.6 Luna, cost about half as much as Haiku ($0.19) and passed 6.3 points more.

On the routing itself, the paper reports that letting the router see the session history, rather than just the latest message, beat the same router without it at the same share of work offloaded to the cheap model. With history, it stayed within one point of always using the expensive model. I'd treat that as one benchmark family on one kind of task, not a general law.

## 3. Character.AI files for Chapter 11 as Google shows up as a creditor, and Disney buys assets for $20M

**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: stakes-first, with a clear line between what filings show and what is inference. Frames not taken: teen-safety lawsuit retrospective; "acqui-hire model is dead" take.

A chatbot company that millions of teenagers used has filed for bankruptcy, and the details of who is owed what say a lot about how AI startups get absorbed by big tech.

Character.AI, which lets people chat with AI characters, filed for Chapter 11 around October 12-13 (outlets differ on the day). Reported liabilities are in the range of $50 million to $100 million. It has agreed to sell certain assets to Disney for $20 million and to give Disney a non-exclusive, perpetual license to its technology, and it says it wants a court-supervised sale by year end to keep the platform running.

Two years ago, Google paid $2.7 billion in a licensing and talent deal that brought co-founders Noam Shazeer and Daniel de Freitas and dozens of staff back to Google. It was not an acquisition. In this filing, Google reportedly appears as a creditor with more than $4 million in claims, alongside about $2.6 million owed to five law firms, according to Business Insider's reading of the petition.

What the reporting shows: the company is in bankruptcy, it faces lawsuits over teen safety (including one by Megan Garcia over the death of her 14-year-old son), and the people who built the technology are largely at Google. What it does not show is why it filed. Linking the lawsuits to the filing is my inference, and the filing itself may not say so. I'd also hold loosely the idea that this proves the hire-the-founders model is risky, since this is one case.
