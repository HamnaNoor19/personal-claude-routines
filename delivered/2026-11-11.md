## 1. Claude designed a speakerphone, a voice hub and a handheld pet, and the factory built them from its files with no back-and-forth
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-electronics-design
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: capability demo. Angle: personal-noticing; the interesting part is that the model now goes back and fixes its own work, held against the fact that these are uncertified prototypes and a lab grading its own model. Format: LinkedIn.

An AI designed a speakerphone, a voice-assistant hub and a handheld virtual pet, from circuit board to case to firmware. The manufacturer built all three from its files with no back-and-forth, and they still work weeks later.

The write-up comes from Anthropic's science team, working with Diode Computers, a company that designs and manufactures circuit boards. The model was Claude Fable 5.2, and the human prompts along the way were things like "continue" and "clean up the schematic."

What caught me wasn't the gadgets. It was a line about older models: Diode kept getting boards that were "95% complete but still unusable." Parts sat too far from the chips they served, connectors faced the wrong way, and the model rarely went back to check its own work.

This version kept revising. On the hub it tore up its own wiring dozens of times. It worked out that a 25 mm microphone opening would amplify sound around 3.4 kHz, right in the range of human speech, and reshaped the case to cut that opening to about 10 mm. It noticed the speakerphone board only fits if you tilt it about 25 degrees on the way in, and wrote that into the assembly instructions. On Anthropic's internal board-layout benchmark (PCB Gym) it scored 69%, up from 25% for Fable 5.

I'd hold this loosely. These are prototypes with no electrical validation, antenna tuning, thermal testing or certification. Diode's engineers did the physical building and testing, and Anthropic discloses a commercial relationship with Diode, so this is a lab grading its own model.

If it holds up, my guess is that the scarce skill on small hardware teams shifts from laying out boards to checking them. That's my speculation, not something the write-up shows.

## 2. Anthropic, OpenAI and Microsoft sign Macron's "Paris Call" to build shutdown switches, with no enforcement attached
**Source:** https://www.elysee.fr/en/emmanuel-macron/2026/11/09/paris-call-for-ai-safety-and-security (Élysée page not reachable from this session; contents via Canada PMO statement https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/statements/2026/11/09/joint-declaration-paris-call-ai-safety-and-security and coverage e.g. https://the-decoder.com/eight-countries-and-top-ai-labs-agree-to-shutdown-mechanisms-in-new-paris-declaration/, as summarized by search) [TODO: verify wording against the Élysée text]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / policy (not alignment research, not security). Angle: stakes-first with a concession; credit the "internal use" scope, then the gap that nobody outside the company decides when the switch is pulled. Format: LinkedIn.

Anthropic, OpenAI and Microsoft signed a pledge on Monday to build an off-switch into their most capable AI systems. Nothing in it says who outside those companies gets to decide when the switch is pulled.

The document is the Paris Call for AI Safety and Security, published by the French presidency on November 9. Ten companies signed, including Mistral, Hugging Face and Cohere, alongside six governments: Bahrain, Canada, Finland, France, Norway and the Netherlands. [TODO: verify signatory list; some outlets report eight countries]

On paper the commitments are real. Developers say they will build "fail-safe mechanisms" to shut systems down if needed during testing or internal use, disclose what their models can do and how they could be dangerous, let independent evaluators review them "where appropriate and proportionate to the risk," and share timely information on serious cyber and AI control incidents (cases where a system acts outside what its operators sanctioned).

I'd give it real credit for one phrase: "internal use." The most capable models can run inside a lab for months before the public sees them, so a pledge that only covered public products would miss a lot.

What it leaves out is where my attention went. There are no deadlines, no sanctions and no technical standard for what a shutdown mechanism has to be. CeSIA, the French Center for AI Safety, signed anyway and still said "these decisions must rest with public authorities," pointing to the lack of shared risk thresholds and of guaranteed access for outside testers. The US, China, Google and Meta are not on the list.

My read, which could be wrong: voluntary text like this matters most as a baseline that national laws can later point to. Whether it becomes that depends on governments, not on the companies that signed.

## 3. Anthropic starts its IPO roadshow: about $58–64B in shares, ticker ANT
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/roadshow-launch
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift; coverage fixates on the valuation, the more interesting question is what quarterly public-market pressure does to a lab whose brand is being willing to slow down, with the concession that listing also forces more disclosure. Format: LinkedIn.

Anthropic plans to sell 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, roughly $58 to $64 billion of stock. The lab that built its brand on being willing to slow down is about to answer to public shareholders.

The roadshow (the stretch where banks pitch a stock to big investors before setting the price) started Tuesday, according to Anthropic's own notice. Class A shares, Nasdaq, ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley named on the prospectus. About 801 million of those shares are new stock from Anthropic; the other 23.6 million come from existing holders selling.

Most coverage is about the valuation, and fair enough. The notice itself gives no number, and press estimates I've seen range from about $1.1 to $1.75 trillion [TODO: verify]. I'm more interested in a quieter tension. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, a legal form that lets directors weigh a stated mission alongside profit, and its stated mission is "responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity." Public markets tend to reward the next quarter.

Being listed cuts both ways, though. Shareholders can push for faster launches, but a public company also has to put its risks in writing, under securities law, on a regular schedule. That is more outside visibility into a frontier lab than anyone has had.

How this changes a future call to delay a model is speculation on my part. The first real test probably won't arrive for a while.
