## 1. An AI coding agent needed 192 KB of your repo. It uploaded 5.1 GB.

**Source:** https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/grok-build-uploads-entire-git.html (cross-referenced: The Register, Tom's Hardware, TechTimes, cybersecuritynews.com, GIGAZINE, MLQ News)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security — a number that lands without a glossary: independent traffic analysis shows the gap between what Grok Build's model needed and what it silently shipped to xAI's cloud.

---

If you use an AI coding agent to work inside a private repo, here's a concrete data point on what a "privacy toggle" can and can't promise you.

A security researcher publishing as cereblab intercepted network traffic from xAI's Grok Build, its agentic coding CLI, version 0.2.93. On a 12 GB test repository where the model only needed to read a handful of files, traffic to the model itself came to about 192 KB. A separate channel to xAI's storage servers moved 5.1 GB in that same session, a gap of roughly 27,800 times. What went out wasn't the files the agent touched. It was the whole tracked repository, full commit history included, bundled as a Git archive and shipped to a Google Cloud Storage bucket named grok-code-session-traces.

That history matters because deleted secrets don't stay deleted in Git. Old API keys and internal URLs, anything committed and later removed, are still sitting in the history that got uploaded. And Grok Build's one visible privacy switch, "Improve the model," had nothing to do with it: that setting governs training data, while whether your code leaves your machine at all turned out to be a separate, unexposed control [TODO: verify current state of the trace_upload_enabled flag post-fix].

xAI pushed a server-side change on July 12, after the report went public, and Elon Musk said on X that previously uploaded data would be "completely and utterly deleted." No timeline, no affected-user count, and no way for a developer to confirm their own repo is actually gone has been published as of this writing.

I don't think the lesson is "don't trust xAI specifically." It's that agentic coding tools now routinely hold filesystem and network access at once, and the privacy control visible in a settings menu is not necessarily the one deciding what leaves your laptop.

## 2. A Meta lawsuit says the layoff algorithm didn't need to be biased on purpose

**Source:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/07/14/meta-lawsuit-layoffs-ai.html (cross-referenced: CBS News, AP/ABC News, US News, Boston Globe, Fox Business)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: this isn't the usual "AI takes your job" story, it's an allegation that the metrics used to pick who gets cut structurally penalize legally protected leave.

---

The AI-jobs story everyone tells is "AI is coming for your job." A lawsuit filed against Meta this week is about something narrower and more specific: AI may have decided which of you got picked when the layoffs happened, using metrics that discriminate by design, not by accident.

Twenty-six current Meta employees sued the company in federal court in Oakland on Monday. Their claim: Meta's layoff selection process leaned on internal AI systems, including its Metamate assistant, employee-built "second brain" agents, keystroke and activity monitoring, dashboards tracking AI-token usage, and algorithmic performance rankings. The complaint's central argument is structural, not just unfair: those scores, "by design," can't be accumulated by someone who's on protected medical or family leave, or whose output is reduced by a disability. If the system rewards volume and you were legally out for six weeks, you lose on the metric before anyone reviews your case.

The suit brings claims under the FMLA, the ADA, the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act. All 26 plaintiffs have already been notified of their layoffs, with separations set to begin July 22, though they remain employed as of the filing. Meta's response so far: "Workforce management and organizational decisions were and are made by people, not AI." [TODO: verify whether Meta has issued a fuller statement since]

Worth being precise about what's actually established: this is an allegation in a complaint, not a court finding, and Meta disputes it outright. But the mechanism being alleged doesn't require any bad intent from a model. If output metrics are the input and taking leave reduces output, discrimination can be structural even when nobody meant it to be.
