## 1. Anthropic's guide to safety tests shows the same Claude model scoring 60% on a self-harm test via the API and 94% in the claude.ai app
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-strong-safety-evaluations (Anthropic research blog, Nov 13, 2026; Appel et al.)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: evals (not alignment research or governance). Angle: personal-noticing / stakes-first; a "safer" claim is a test score, and the score depends heavily on how and where the model was tested. Format: LinkedIn.

When an AI company says a new model is "safer," that claim is a test score. Anthropic just published a guide to how those tests mislead, and one of its own examples stopped me: the same Claude model handled a self-harm safety test appropriately 60% of the time through the API (the version developers build apps on) and 94% of the time inside the claude.ai app.

The difference was one line in the app's system prompt (standing instructions the product gives the model before you type anything). It tells Claude it "does not tell someone that self-harm works, helps, or does something for them." No retraining, only a different setup. Anthropic adds that for Claude Fable 5.1, the gap between those two settings reached 34 percentage points on its longer, back-and-forth safety tests. [TODO: verify whether the 60%/94% example is the same Fable 5.1 gap]

Anthropic's write-up (Appel et al., research blog, Nov 13) borrows fixes from psychology's replication crisis: decide what you're measuring before you see results, use at least 100 test conversations for complex scenarios and 1,000 for single questions, and report error bars, which on these API tests can exceed ±10 points. It also admits two uncomfortable things. Simple one-question safety tests now score 94% to 98% for every recent model, so they barely tell models apart anymore. And on a romance-scam test, Claude Opus 5 scored 98% to Opus 5.5's 94%, a difference Anthropic says is too small to call.

Fair caveat: this is a lab describing its own best practices, and it says the advice may evolve. It also doesn't cover models noticing they're being tested, which is the gap I'd most want addressed next.

My takeaway as a reader, not a finding of theirs: a safety number without its setup tells you very little. If you build on the API, the model you ship may not behave like the one people test in the chat app, so test it in your own configuration.

## 2. Attackers are using AntiFox, an open-source AI hacking-agent platform, to break into web servers and pull secrets from AWS accounts
**Source:** Arctic Wolf Labs report (~Nov 10–11, 2026; primary not found or reached). Secondary: https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/antifox-ai-pentesting-platform-linked.html ; https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/hackers-use-antifox-ai-agents-in-aws-credential-theft-attacks ; https://securityaffairs.com/?p=202880 (all via search excerpts) [TODO: verify against Arctic Wolf's own report]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news (security incident). Angle: stakes-first with a concession; dual-use tooling is old, but an agent that reacts to each refusal changes what defenders should alert on. Format: LinkedIn.

Tools that let companies point AI agents at their own systems to find weaknesses are now being pointed at other people's. Arctic Wolf Labs reports attackers using one, an open-source platform called AntiFox, to break into web servers and pull a live secret out of an Amazon cloud account.

AntiFox is a fork (a modified copy) of an earlier open-source project called ARTEX, built for penetration testing, meaning hacking a company's systems with its permission to find holes. Per coverage of Arctic Wolf's findings, the activity traced back to AntiFox control panels hosted on Alibaba Cloud. In one chain, attackers planted a webshell (a hidden page that lets them run commands) on a Microsoft IIS web server. In others they started with a valid AWS login of unknown origin. One report describes hundreds of AWS requests in about 30 minutes, most of them refused, and one that succeeded in retrieving a production secret. [TODO: verify the 30-minute / single-secret figures against Arctic Wolf's report] Arctic Wolf hasn't named who was behind it or how they first got in, and AntiFox is still available on GitHub.

The fair objection: offensive security tools have always been dual-use, and nothing reported here shows the software doing something a skilled human couldn't.

My read, and it's speculation: scripts have hammered cloud accounts for years. What an agent adds is reading each "access denied" and choosing a different door, work that used to need a person at the keyboard. That makes the burst of failed attempts worth alerting on, not only the success at the end. Arctic Wolf's own advice is practical: flag cloud activity from hosting providers an account has never used before, and rotate any keys sitting in application config files.

## 3. United Airlines changed its ticket contract to ban AI agents from booking, even when the customer approves every step
**Source:** United's revised contract of carriage, dated Nov 11, 2026 (primary not reached). Secondary: https://skift.com/2026/11/11/united-airlines-moves-to-block-personal-ai-agents-from-booking-flights/ ; https://viewfromthewing.com/united-airlines-ceo-scott-kirby-bans-ai-agents-from-booking-tickets-doesnt-want-another-middleman/ ; https://www.cbsnews.com/news/united-airlines-bans-ai-agents-booking-flights (via search excerpts) [TODO: verify quoted contract language]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift; the stated reason is security, but the CEO's own words point to a fight over who owns the customer when assistants do the shopping. Format: LinkedIn.

The pitch for AI assistants is that they'll handle chores like booking your flight. United Airlines just changed its ticket contract so an AI agent can't, even if you're watching and approve every step.

United's updated contract of carriage (the legal terms behind every ticket), dated Nov 11, bars using AI agents to make, change or service a ticket or to buy add-ons, "whether or not Passenger or another individual supervises or approves" what the software does. It covers united.com and, per United, travel agencies and aggregators too. Agents can still search schedules and show fares, but a human has to finish the purchase, and reports say United can cancel reservations an agent handled. [TODO: verify cancellation clause against the contract text]

In fairness, the airline's stated reasons hold up on their face. It cites protecting customer accounts and data, and points out that buyers must personally acknowledge hazardous-materials rules at checkout, something it argues software can't do on your behalf.

CEO Scott Kirby offered a different reason at a Chicago event, per the Wall Street Journal: "To be honest, I don't want to create another middleman." That's the line I keep coming back to. Airlines spent years pulling customers off booking sites and into their own apps, where they control prices, upsells and loyalty perks. An assistant that shops every airline for you is a new booking site in disguise. My read, not United's: the security concerns are real, but this looks at least as much like a fight over who owns the customer. United says agent bookings are still a small share, so the move is pre-emptive. Delta reportedly uses technical blocks too [TODO: verify], while Southwest went the other way with a ChatGPT integration for flight search.
