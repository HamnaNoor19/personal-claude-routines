Your city's permit office or your state's benefits agency can now buy Claude at 40% off list price. Until now that discount was reserved for federal agencies, and the $1-per-agency promotional deal that started it ends tomorrow, October 31.

That's the part I'd pay attention to. A $1 price is a trial, not a price. The 40% discount is the first number that says what governments will actually pay for this stuff. Per the coverage I read, it applies to Claude's token-based usage (tokens are the chunks of text a model reads and writes, which is how AI usage gets billed), has no minimum spend, and extends for the first time to state, local, tribal and territorial governments. One report says it runs about 27 months, to January 2029. [TODO: verify term]

Government buyers get used to a price quickly, and the labs now have a reason to compete on whatever sits under the discount, like security approvals and support, rather than headline rates.

## 2. Opera proposes WebAI, a web standard so websites can use an AI model running on your own device
**Source:** https://www.androidheadlines.com/2026/10/opera-webai-local-browser-ai-launch.html (also TechCrunch, 9to5Mac, Unite.AI coverage; primary Opera post and standard text not found). Announced Oct 27. [TODO: verify Google/Microsoft backing, API count]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn (assumed). Frame: product launch / dev tooling. Angle: frame-shift; this is less about Opera's browser and more about who pays for AI features inside websites. Frames not taken: privacy explainer, browser wars.

Every AI feature on a website today has a bill behind it, because someone is paying a cloud provider each time you click "summarize." Opera's new WebAI proposal tries to remove that bill by letting the site use a model that already lives on your computer.

Opera announced it on October 27 as a proposed web standard, in early preview in Opera One, with Opera GX and Neon to follow. Websites and browser extensions would call a model running locally, such as Google's Gemma (an open-weight model, meaning its trained parameters are downloadable), or on Macs Apple's on-device model. Coverage says the launch includes three new developer APIs, and that Google and Microsoft back the proposal. Users have to opt in, and Opera says locally handled data stays on the device. [TODO: verify]

For a small developer, the appeal is obvious: add a summarizer or translator without running servers or paying per request. The catch Opera itself notes is that results depend on your hardware, and a local model will load down your machine. A 2022 laptop and a new one will behave very differently, and the site can't control that.

I'd also hold back on "standard." A proposal from one browser vendor is not the same as the others shipping it, and I couldn't find the spec text itself. What I'd watch is whether Chrome or Safari ship anything compatible, since that decides whether developers can rely on it.

Anthropic has stopped giving free Claude credits to scientists one at a time and started handing them to the organizations that fund scientists. On October 28 it named 20 foundations, institutions and research groups in an expansion of its AI for Science program, which began in May 2025.

According to one secondary breakdown, which I couldn't check against the full list, the 20 split into six funders and societies, six institutions, six research projects, and two earlier collaborations (HHMI Janelia and the Allen Institute). The funders reportedly include the National Science Foundation and the Simons and Sloan foundations, and they would pass credits on to the researchers they already support. [TODO: verify names]

The logic is easy to see. A funder already knows which labs are doing good work, and Anthropic skips the job of picking winners. It also puts Claude in front of researchers in the setting where they decide which tools to adopt.

What I can't tell from what I read is the size of the credits, how many researchers get them, or what outcome counts as success. Free credits are an adoption tool. They tell you where Anthropic wants to be used, not whether it speeds up any science. I'd be more convinced by the first paper that credits a result to this program than by the partner list.
