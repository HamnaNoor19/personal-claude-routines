## 1. OpenAI wouldn't ship a model over cyber risk. Three days later it shipped a narrower one instead.

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/responding-next-frontier-critical-cyber-capabilities/ (Astra) and https://openai.com/index/expanding-daybreak-as-the-cyber-defense-window-narrows/ (Daybreak / GPT-5.6-Cyber), cross-referenced with https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/07/openai-says-it-slowed-astra-model-development-over-security-concerns/ and https://www.axios.com/2026/08/10/openai-gpt-astra-restrictions-safety-hacking-defenders
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability / dangerous-capability evals — frame-shift: coverage is reading GPT-5.6-Cyber as "OpenAI ships a hacking model," but the sharper story is the gating decision behind it, a lab publicly refusing to release a model over a named, specific risk for the first time.

On August 7, OpenAI said something no major AI lab has said in public before: it can't rule out that an unreleased internal model, called Astra, has crossed into what the company calls "Critical" cyber capability under its internal risk framework (the Preparedness Framework). In plain terms, that's the tier where a model could find and exploit security holes (zero-days, meaning bugs nobody has patched yet) in well-defended, real-world systems on its own, without a human walking it through each step.

OpenAI didn't conclude Astra definitely clears that bar. Its internal evaluations and outside red-teamers just couldn't rule it out, which was apparently enough to pause non-compliant internal work on the model, wall off testing, and add monitors that watch the model's reasoning for risky or misaligned steps in real time.

Three days later, on August 10, OpenAI shipped something adjacent: GPT-5.6-Cyber, a version of its GPT-5.6 model trained to refuse far fewer requests tied to offensive security work, exploit-chain development, breaking authentication, escalating privileges. In OpenAI's own testing, GPT-5.6-Cyber complied with 95% of advanced cybersecurity requests, versus about 1.5% for the standard public model [TODO: verify exact percentages against OpenAI's post directly]. It's only available to vetted defenders through an expanded program called Daybreak, and the company says it reaches just the tier below Astra's, "High," not "Critical."

I don't think the headline here is "OpenAI built a hacking model." Frontier labs have been building offense-capable systems for evals on purpose for a while now. What stopped me was the sequencing: a lab said no to shipping something because its own tests couldn't clear it of a specific, named danger, then shipped a deliberately narrower sibling two days later to a gated audience. That's the release-gating process actually doing something, for once, instead of reading like a policy document nobody tests against.

I'd hold the applause on one point. The entity grading Astra as "can't rule out Critical" and GPT-5.6-Cyber as "only High" is OpenAI itself. A passing internal eval is evidence about what a model does under OpenAI's own testing conditions, not an independent safety guarantee. Whether outside groups get to check that grading matters at least as much as the grade.

## 2. Anthropic just bought $9.1 billion of power from a Bitcoin miner

**Source:** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-11/anthropic-strikes-9-billion-deal-with-cloud-computing-firm-riot, cross-referenced with https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/article/riot-platforms-stock-surges-on-91-billion-data-center-deal-with-anthropic-132120194.html and https://qz.com/anthropic-riot-platforms-data-center-deal-9-billion-081126
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: this reads as a compute story, but it's really an energy-market story; Bitcoin miners already own the grid connections and land AI labs now need.

If someone told you a year ago that a Bitcoin mining company would become a major supplier to a frontier AI lab, that would have sounded backwards. It just happened anyway.

Anthropic signed a $9.1 billion deal with Riot Platforms, a company whose main business until recently was mining Bitcoin, on August 11. Riot will supply Anthropic 191 megawatts of power capacity, roughly enough electricity to run 143,000 homes at once, from its Rockdale, Texas site. The lease runs 20 years, through 2048, with two five-year extension options that could push the total value to $16.1 billion. Riot's stock jumped roughly 25% after hours on the news.

This is Anthropic's third major compute deal in three months, and together they add up to more than $60 billion in commitments [TODO: verify aggregate figure]. The pattern says something: the bottleneck for frontier AI right now isn't just chips, it's electricity, land, and grid connections, the unglamorous infrastructure that takes years to permit and build. Bitcoin miners happen to already own a lot of that. They picked sites for cheap power near transmission lines, built the substations, and now that crypto mining margins have compressed, they have spare capacity and an obvious new customer.

None of this capacity shows up overnight. Riot's rollout is staged: 96 megawatts online by December 2027, the full 191 by June 2028. Anthropic is buying power years ahead of needing it, essentially treating electricity like the strategic reserve it has become for the industry.

The part I keep coming back to isn't the dollar figure, it's what it implies about the shape of the AI race going forward. If compute increasingly means "who can lock down power first," the companies with the best chips don't automatically win. The ones with the best power contracts might.

## 3. A 150-million-parameter model that reasons without ever writing down a thought

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09888, cross-referenced with https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-news/pathway-claims-11x-lower-ai-reasoning-costs-with-150m-parameter-model and https://www.thedeepview.com/articles/pathway-breakthrough-challenges-ai-economics
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Technique / pattern — niche concept made legible: most "reasoning" models think out loud in visible text; this one reasons silently inside a compressed memory state, and that's what makes it radically cheaper.

Most AI models that "reason" do it by writing out their thinking in plain text, paragraphs of "let's break this down, step one, step two." That text is expensive: every reasoning token you generate, you pay for and wait for. A new paper from the AI lab Pathway, called BDH-CQ, tries something different: a 150-million-parameter model (tiny; GPT-5.6-class models are almost certainly hundreds of times larger) that reasons without writing any of it down.

Instead of generating a visible chain of thought, BDH-CQ solves problems by updating an internal, compressed memory state as it reads examples, then working the answer out through repeated internal computation, never producing intermediate text at all. Think of it as reasoning that happens entirely in the model's head, rather than reasoning it narrates out loud.

The result that's making people pay attention: on ARC-AGI-1, a benchmark of visual pattern puzzles specifically designed to resist memorization, BDH-CQ scores 29.5% pass@2 (correct within two tries) at a computed cost of $0.0007 per task, under a tenth of a cent. Researchers are calling that a new low-cost point on the accuracy-per-dollar curve for this benchmark.

Worth being precise about what that claim covers and what it doesn't. BDH-CQ is not more accurate than the field's best. GPT-5.6-class models score north of 90% on the same benchmark, and even a mid-tier model like GPT-5.6 Luna beats BDH-CQ's accuracy outright, 34.2% versus 29.5%, while costing about 11 times more per task [TODO: verify Luna comparison figures]. What BDH-CQ demonstrates is that you can get meaningfully non-trivial reasoning performance at a cost point nothing else has reached, not that it's the smartest model on the leaderboard.

That distinction is the actually interesting part. Whether a technique that reasons silently in latent space keeps this cost advantage as it scales up is unproven. But it points at a different tradeoff than "bigger model, more visible reasoning tokens": cheap-and-decent might end up mattering more than smart-and-expensive for a lot of real applications.
