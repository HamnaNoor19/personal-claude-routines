## 1. A federal judge just ruled the Pentagon broke the law punishing Anthropic for refusing an unrestricted deal

**Source:** https://www.nbcnews.com/business/business-news/anthropic-pentagon-blacklist-claude-judge-rcna594825 (NBC News, published Aug 28)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business, with legal/policy stakes. Frames not taken: governance/policy deep-dive, alignment framing. Angle: frame-shift — most coverage will read this as "Anthropic wins a lawsuit"; the sharper story is the first real court test of whether an AI company can hold a usage red line against its own government customer.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift.

---

A federal judge just ruled the U.S. government broke the law by punishing Anthropic for refusing an unrestricted deal with the Pentagon.

The dispute goes back to negotiations over how the military could use Claude. Anthropic wanted guardrails written in: no fully autonomous weapons, no domestic mass surveillance. The Pentagon wanted the opposite, unrestricted access to Claude for any lawful purpose, with no carve-outs. When Anthropic held its line and said so publicly, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's department designated the company a "supply chain risk," a label from an obscure federal procurement statute normally reserved for firms suspected of being compromised by a foreign adversary. It was the first time any U.S. company had been hit with that label in public.

On Thursday, U.S. District Judge Rita Lin struck the designation down. In a 59-page order, she wrote that "the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics." She found that the government's "words and deeds confirm that the challenged actions were based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government," and called the designation arbitrary, capricious, and a violation of the First Amendment and due process.

Strip away the legal language and the question underneath is fairly direct: can an agency use a label meant for actual national-security threats to punish a vendor for refusing to hand over its product with no restrictions attached? A court just said no, at least in this instance. My guess is it won't be the last case like it. Every AI company selling into government contracts right now is watching how much room they actually have to keep their own usage limits when the customer holding the leverage is the government itself.

## 2. Google DeepMind ran a safety test where neither side could see the other's cards

**Source:** https://deepmind.google/blog/piloting-the-worlds-first-double-blind-ai-evaluations/ (Google DeepMind blog, published Aug 27)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability / dangerous-capability evals (evaluation methodology and infrastructure). Frames not taken: alignment, interpretability, governance/policy. Angle: stakes-first — the trust problem in third-party safety evaluation of proprietary models, and a technical fix for it.

Frame: evals. Angle: stakes-first.

---

Every safety benchmark for a company's own AI model runs into the same trust problem. If the company that owns the model also picks or previews the test questions, a passing score doesn't tell you much: the model could just be prepared for exactly that test. The obvious fix, handing the questions to an outside evaluator, creates a mirror problem: now the evaluator needs the model's weights, the actual trained parameters that make up the model, and handing those over means giving away the product itself.

Google DeepMind just piloted a way around both sides of that: what they're calling the first double-blind evaluation, testing set up so neither party can see the other's cards, of a proprietary, frontier-class model. Working with the Singapore AI Safety Institute, the nonprofit OpenMined, evaluator AVERI, and standards group MLCommons, they ran both the model and the test questions inside an encrypted environment neither side could see into. AVERI never had access to Gemini's weights, and Google never saw AVERI's questions. Cryptographic proof, mathematical evidence that can't be faked or forged after the fact, confirmed afterward that the agreed model and the agreed test set were actually the ones used.

The pilot ran on Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, tested against held-out questions from MLCommons' AILuminate benchmark, covering things like cyberattack assistance, chemical and biological weapon information, hate speech, self-harm content, and violent-crime instructions. Worth being precise about what this shows and doesn't. It's a working proof that double-blind evaluation is technically possible for a proprietary model, run on a lighter model rather than a flagship, and a single pilot rather than a standard anyone else has adopted yet. It doesn't mean this model is safe in some general sense, only that this particular test happened under conditions where neither side could quietly tilt the result.

## 3. A $20,000 humanoid robot can now be taken over by anyone in Bluetooth range, and the exploit spreads itself

**Source:** https://boschko.ca/g1-ble-rce/ (researcher disclosure, published Aug 27)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / AI-robotics security. Frames not taken: technique/pattern explainer, hands-on observation. Angle: frame-shift — this reads in most coverage as "another IoT bug"; the sharper point is that AI-powered humanoid robots inherit every physical-security problem of embedded IoT devices, at human scale, and this one self-propagates.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift.

---

A $20,000 AI-powered humanoid robot can now be fully taken over by anyone who walks within Bluetooth range of it, no password required, and the exploit spreads itself to the next one it meets.

Security researcher Boschko published two vulnerabilities this week in Unitree's G1 EDU, a roughly $20,000 humanoid robot used in university and industry robotics labs. One is a fairly ordinary web bug: a flaw in the file-upload feature for the robot's onboard AI assistant lets an attacker write a file into a folder the robot will execute as root, the highest level of system access, handing over full control from anywhere on the network. The other one is the part that stopped me.

The robot exposes a Bluetooth channel, a short-range wireless radio, that accepted write commands with no pairing and no authentication at all: any nearby device could just talk to it. From there, a plaintext request returns the robot's encryption key wrapped in another layer of encryption meant to protect it in transit. That protection turned out not to matter, because Unitree's own cloud service would unwrap that key for any registered Unitree account, without checking whether the account actually owned that specific robot. Put those two gaps together and anyone with any Unitree account can pull any G1's key out of the air and get root, from Bluetooth range, with no login on the robot itself. The researcher calls it wormable: a compromised robot can pass the same attack on to the next G1 that wanders into range.

Two things temper this. The BLE exploit still needs physical proximity, it isn't reachable over the open internet, and Unitree has already patched the cloud-side ownership check as of July, closing half the chain; the researcher received a $6,700 bounty for the pair of bugs. What's not yet clear is whether the on-robot firmware fix for the BLE side has fully shipped. Robotics companies are racing to put AI decision-making into machines that move through physical space, and that race is outpacing the far less glamorous work, pairing requirements, account-ownership checks, that the rest of the IoT industry learned the hard way over the last decade.
