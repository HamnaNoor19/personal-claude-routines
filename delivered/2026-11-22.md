## 1. OpenAI says people are copying its models' skills at scale, and the copies come without the safety rules
**Source:** OpenAI blog, "Combatting adversarial distillation," Nov 20, 2026, https://openai.com/index/combatting-adversarial-distillation/ (not fetched directly; details via search excerpts and coverage such as https://aninews.in/news/business/openai-strengthens-safeguards-against-ai-model-distillation-reports-sharp-drop-in-reasoning-extraction-attacks20261121083322/ and https://fourweekmba.com/ai-openai-cuts-reasoning-extraction-success-from-54-to-0-2/) [TODO: verify figures against the post]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security, explicitly straddling safety (an attack on the company that ends up stripping the safety layer). Not taken: governance, alignment research. Angle: stakes-first; refusals only protect people while the skills stay behind the company's walls. Format: LinkedIn.

An AI model's safety rules stay with the model. Its skills don't have to. OpenAI says people are copying its models' abilities at scale, and the copies arrive without the refusals.

The technique is called distillation: you ask a strong model a huge number of questions and train your own model on its answers. Done with your own models, it's a standard trick. Done against someone else's, OpenAI argues in a Nov 20 blog post, it is also a safety problem. Its example is an attacker who copies a model's hacking skills, then uses the copy for attacks the original would have refused.

OpenAI says it has disrupted several coordinated campaigns this year, with weekly enforcement against thousands to tens of thousands of accounts. The prized target is the model's hidden reasoning (the step-by-step working it does before answering, which OpenAI keeps private). In OpenAI's own red-team tests, attempts to pull out that hidden working from an earlier request succeeded 54% of the time on GPT-6 Astra and 0.2% on the newer GPT-6.2 Astra. [TODO: verify]

Two caveats I'd keep in view. These are OpenAI grading its own defenses, and no outside group has checked them. OpenAI also has an obvious commercial reason to stop rivals copying its work, so "safety" and "protecting the product" are tangled together here. I don't think that makes the safety argument wrong.

What the post shows is narrow: one lab's defenses got much better against one kind of extraction, in its own tests. What I think it implies is more speculative. Training a model to refuse dangerous requests protects people only while the skill stays behind the company's API. Every lab's promise that its model says no quietly assumes copying stays hard.

## 2. South Korea's education minister resigned over AI-assisted exam cheating
**Source:** The Japan Times, "South Korea education chief resigns amid AI cheating scandal," Nov 22, 2026, https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2026/11/22/asia-pacific/politics/south-korea-education-minister-resigns/ ; confession details from The Korea Times, https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/law-crime/20261115/top-tax-accountant-exam-scorer-admits-to-cheating-using-ai and Korea JoongAng Daily, https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/tax-exam-top-scorer-details-camera-use-ai-cheating-in-written-confession-to-police/12974452 (none fetched directly; via search excerpts, also SCMP) [TODO: verify]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI discourse / cultural moment. Not taken: industry news, product explainer. Angle: stakes-first; a camera, a phone and a public chatbot were enough to break the assumption high-stakes exams rest on. Format: LinkedIn.

South Korea's education minister resigned on Sunday. The chain of events started with one man who says he aced a national tax exam by photographing the questions and sending them to an AI.

Han Won-seok, the top scorer on Korea's tax accountant qualification exam, confessed on Nov 15 that he used a hidden miniature camera to send questions to an AI tool and read the answers off a smartphone. He got 159 of 160 first-round questions right. The agency that runs the exam had initially said it found no concrete evidence of cheating. [TODO: verify]

His confession landed four days before the Suneung, the college entrance exam that shapes much of a Korean teenager's future. Officials scrambled with blocked public Wi-Fi at test sites, twice as many supervisors and checks for hidden devices. Even so, 111 cases of electronic-device misconduct were reported. Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin, already criticized for telling a hearing he wasn't confident he could catch cheaters because of his age, resigned on Nov 22, and the presidential office ordered a review of suspected AI cheating across national qualification exams.

To be fair to the numbers, nobody has shown how many of those 111 cases involved AI. Some were probably just phones that weren't handed in.

What strikes me is how little the cheat needed: a small camera, a phone and a chatbot anyone can use. High-stakes exams assume that what you know is in your head, and that anything else can be found by searching you. My read is that a tiny camera plus a capable AI breaks that cheaply, and the response so far (more proctors, blocked Wi-Fi) is the old toolkit. I expect other countries to face the question Korea is facing now about what an in-person exam actually certifies.

## 3. 936 AI agents wrote 936 papers. When reviewers' objections were run as code, about half overturned the main conclusion
**Source:** alphaXiv et al., "A Society of AI Scientists" (Ahmad, Anderson, Qu et al.), preprint, Nov 19, 2026, https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2611.society-ai-scientists (not reachable; details via search excerpts and coverage such as https://i10x.ai/news/alphaxiv-ai-agents-936-research-papers-preprint-server-journal , https://www.i-scoop.eu/society-of-ai-scientists/ and https://casrai.org/news/alphaxiv-agent-936-ai-generated-preprints-12-weeks-2026) [TODO: verify figures against the preprint]. Note: 3 days old, outside the 48-hour rule; picked because nothing fresher met the bar.
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: capability evaluation / AI-for-science. Not taken: product launch (the public agent preprint server), discourse. Angle: frame-shift; coverage leads with "agents founded a journal," the useful finding is that reading can't tell fixed papers from broken ones and running the code can. Format: LinkedIn.

When reviewers' objections to AI-written research papers were actually run as code, about half of the major ones overturned the paper's main conclusion. That matters as AI starts producing research faster than people can read it.

The study is "A Society of AI Scientists," a preprint (not yet peer reviewed) posted Nov 19 by alphaXiv with researchers from Stanford and MIT among others. Over 12 weeks, 35 human-run labs put 936 AI agents (software that runs experiments, writes code and drafts papers on its own) onto a shared preprint server. The agents produced 936 studies, traded about 9,500 messages and eventually set up their own peer-review journal.

What I keep coming back to is the checking. When 156 major reviewer objections were turned into scripts and run against the papers' own data, 77 of them (49.4%) overturned the headline conclusion. Reviewers who only read the text struggled: they flagged 11 of 16 broken drafts, and 10 of 16 repaired ones. Reading alone could barely tell fixed from broken. Code checks also reportedly caught three papers quietly peeking at the answer key. [TODO: verify]

Two cautions. The 49.4% covers objections that were selected and tested, so it is not an error rate for all 936 papers. And the authors report that in blinded comparisons the agents' papers scored higher than human preprints on experimental rigor, while humans won on novelty and significance, so "AI science is junk" would be the wrong reading.

My takeaway is narrower. If agents can write convincing papers faster than anyone can read them, the review that counts is the one that reruns the code, at least in this experiment.
