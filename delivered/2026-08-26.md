## 1. OpenAI's first custom chip claims it beats Nvidia's Blackwell on inference

**Source:** https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/openai-jalapeno-better-than-nvidia
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business (infrastructure) — frame-shift: not "OpenAI made a chip," but an early, partially-independent data point on whether Nvidia's grip on AI's recurring cost (inference) is cracking.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / infrastructure. Angle: frame-shift with an explicit fairness caveat.

---

OpenAI just published benchmark numbers claiming its first custom chip beats Nvidia's currently-shipping AI hardware at the job that actually decides what your ChatGPT subscription costs: running the model, not training it.

The chip is called Jalapeño, built with Broadcom, and it's designed only for inference (the process of a trained model actually answering your prompt), not training. Training is the expensive one-time cost of building a model. Inference is the cost that recurs every single time someone uses it, which is most of what a $20-a-month subscription or an API bill is actually paying for.

SemiAnalysis ran the numbers on its own InferenceX benchmark and verified some of the tests on-site. Across three real open-weight models, GPT-OSS 120B, DeepSeek R1 670B, and Kimi K2.5, Jalapeño delivered 1.5 to 1.9 times more AI work per watt than the best Blackwell systems currently on the market, with 1.7 to 3.6 times lower latency and up to 4 times faster response for interactive use.

One caveat matters before updating your priors on Nvidia's dominance. Jalapeño isn't shipping yet, deployment is slated for late 2026, and these are engineering samples. It also uses newer HBM4 memory, which Nvidia's current Blackwell doesn't have. SemiAnalysis itself called the comparison "somewhat incomplete," since Nvidia's own upcoming Rubin chips use that same memory type. So this measures OpenAI's newest hardware against Nvidia's current lineup, not Nvidia's actual answer to it.

Still, inference is the cost that never goes away once a model ships, and it's the number every AI company's margins live or die on. If even a fraction of these gains hold up once Jalapeño is running in production instead of a lab, that's a real crack in Nvidia's grip on how much AI costs to run.

## 2. Amazon is shutting down Mechanical Turk after 21 years

**Source:** https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/25/amazon-service-that-jeff-bezos-called-artificial-ai-is-shutting-down.html
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — stakes-first: the platform that quietly supplied AI's human training-data labor is closing just as a more specialized version of that same market booms.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: stakes-first with a synthesis close.

---

Amazon is closing Mechanical Turk on September 30, ending the platform that quietly trained a good chunk of the AI systems you use today.

MTurk launched in 2005, and Jeff Bezos once called it "artificial artificial intelligence," because it let companies pay a global pool of freelance workers, at its peak more than 500,000 of them, to do small tasks a computer couldn't do yet: labeling images, transcribing audio, judging whether a sentence made sense. Those labeled examples became exactly the training data that early machine learning, and later large language models, needed to learn from.

The shutdown is total. Amazon confirmed MTurk closes for good on September 30, alongside two related products, SageMaker Ground Truth and Augmented AI, meaning Amazon is exiting the business of humans labeling AI data entirely. It had already stopped taking new customers back in July.

What's actually interesting is the timing. MTurk isn't dying because AI stopped needing human input, it's dying because the market for that input got more specialized. A newer wave of platforms, Scale AI, Mercor, and Prolific among them, do the same basic job with vetted, often expert workers doing higher-value work: rating chatbot answers, writing code review notes, judging whether a model's response is actually correct. The commodity version of human-in-the-loop AI work is getting replaced by a higher-skill version of the same job, not by no job at all.

If your picture of "AI training data" is just scraped text, this is a reminder that a lot of what makes these models usable, the labeling, the ranking, the "is this answer good" judgment, has always come from paid human labor. That labor isn't disappearing. It just moved, and it pays differently now.

## 3. Reddit reportedly lost 86% of its ChatGPT citations, and nobody can explain why

**Source:** https://searchengineland.com/reddit-chatgpt-search-citations-fall-report-485473
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI discourse / cultural moment — skeptical pushback: the scary number is real, but the tidy explanation circulating this week doesn't hold up, and this isn't even the first time.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: AI discourse. Angle: skeptical pushback on a tidy explanation.

---

Reddit's visibility inside ChatGPT reportedly fell 86% in a week. If you're building a business strategy around getting cited by AI search, the scarier part isn't the number, it's that nobody, including OpenAI, can explain it.

A marketing analytics firm called Promptwatch tracks what share of ChatGPT's search citations point to which websites. It found Reddit averaged 3.83% of all ChatGPT Search citations between July 18 and August 7, then dropped to a 0.52% average between August 14 and 17, an 86% relative fall, right as Reddit had just joined the S&P 500.

The popular explanation doesn't quite fit the timing. Most coverage pointed to a change on August 8, when ChatGPT started scoping far more of its background searches directly to specific sites, jumping from 0.4% to almost 17% of its queries, instead of searching the open web generally. Tidy story, except the sharp Reddit drop didn't happen until August 14, six days later. And a different analytics vendor, Ahrefs, measured Reddit's share over the same window at 16.7%, four times Promptwatch's number. Two firms measuring the same thing landed nowhere near each other.

Worth remembering this movie already played once. In September 2025, Reddit's ChatGPT citation share fell from roughly 7% to roughly 1%, just as dramatic a collapse, and it fully recovered to around 3% within months. Reddit didn't change anything either time; something in how ChatGPT decides what to search and cite just shifted, quietly, with no public explanation from OpenAI.

What's actually notable here is that one company's unannounced tweak to a search algorithm can erase most of a major platform's AI visibility overnight, without warning. Anyone building a strategy around "getting cited by ChatGPT" is building on ground that can move without notice.
