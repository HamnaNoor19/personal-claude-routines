## 1. Claude traces the "mystery seeds" behind NIST's standard elliptic curves
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/ecdsa-curve-origins
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: model capability / hands-on result. Angle: stakes-first, with skeptical scoping (real result, narrow, self-reported).

For about 25 years, the standard signature curves used across much of the internet have come with numbers the NSA supplied and never explained. Cryptographers have long asked whether those numbers hide a backdoor.

On Friday Anthropic published a write-up saying one of its internal research models worked out where the numbers came from in under half an hour. The employee who asked didn't mention the popular story (a joke passphrase, which turned out to be wrong). The "seeds" aren't hash outputs, as the standard implied. They come from lrand48, a basic random number generator from 1990s C libraries, started from a Unix timestamp. The ten prime-curve seeds map to dates between May 1997 and April 1998.

The detective work is the fun part. Four of the ten seeds had a zero in the top bit of all 20 of their 32-bit chunks, which would happen by chance about 1 time in 5,810. That fingerprint matched a quirk of lrand48. The model then recovered the generator's hidden state with a lattice-reduction technique and ran it backwards to the timestamps.

My read: a real result, but a narrow one. It isn't a break in ECDSA. A dull, reproducible origin story makes a deliberate backdoor less plausible, though the authors themselves say some valid candidates were skipped for reasons nobody knows, and the starting points for P-256 and several other curves are still unexplained. It's also Anthropic reporting on its own model, so I'd like outside cryptographers to rerun the published code, which regenerates all ten seeds.

What I keep coming back to is that a bounty and decades of amateur effort didn't crack this, and a model did it before lunch.

## 2. Anthropic embeds outside evaluators (METR) inside the company
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/an-update-on-independent-evaluators
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / policy. Angle: stakes-first take with skeptical scoping (who checks the lab's homework, and what the redaction carve-outs mean).

Right now, when an AI lab says its model is safe, the main evidence is the lab's own report. On Thursday Anthropic described a step toward changing that: six employees from METR (a nonprofit that tests frontier models for dangerous capabilities) now have employee-like access, with company hardware, office space, and internal tools roughly on par with Anthropic's own risk assessment teams.

The part I care about most is publication. Anthropic says it cannot veto what METR publishes. It can require redaction of three predefined categories: customer data, attorney-client privileged material, and model architecture details. Anthropic's post lays out three access tiers. In the lowest, Anthropic decides access and must approve publication. METR's arrangement sits in the top tier, where evaluators can say whether they thought a redaction was justified.

I think this is a meaningful move, and I also think the limits deserve equal weight. It is voluntary. It is one small team, walled off from the rest of METR under enforceable agreements. Anthropic picks its partners. And "architecture details" is a broad bucket that could hide things a risk assessment needs. The post doesn't say how disputes over redactions get resolved. ## 3. Anthropic's own data: junior share of technical hires fell from 27% to 9%
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/employment-at-anthropic
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business (first-party data). Angle: stakes-first, skeptical of generalizing from one company that sells the tool.

Anthropic studied its own workforce and published the numbers: the junior share of its technical hires fell from 27% to 9%. If you've wondered what heavy AI use does to entry-level jobs, this is one company's answer, from the company selling the tool.

Other figures from the same report: junior share of technical departments fell from 21% to 14% since early 2025. Code commits per engineer are about 5 times what they were six months earlier. Agent work hours passed human work hours in March 2026, and by August agents logged about six hours per human hour for individual employees.

The detail I found most interesting is a bottleneck. About 80% of interactive Claude session time is idle, waiting on a human. The report says reading everything those sessions produce would take over half a work week. Meetings went up too, with median meeting time rising 20%.

I'd hold this loosely. The authors flag that tool adoption wasn't random, that Anthropic's rapid growth confounds any AI effect, and that it is a single, unusual company with broad employee stock ownership. The quality checks (code reverts, bug-fix deletions) are proxies. And the report is self-authored. It also doesn't claim Anthropic's own commitments would reverse the decline in junior hiring, and calls for government policy on retraining and income support.

Still, a lab publishing uncomfortable numbers about itself is rarer than it should be, and 27% to 9% is a number a hiring manager can picture.
