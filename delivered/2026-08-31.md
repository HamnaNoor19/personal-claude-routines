## 1. Anthropic is signing Claude users out after malware started hijacking their login sessions

**Source:** https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/artificial-intelligence/anthropic-warns-infostealer-malware-is-hijacking-claude-sessions-to-drain-usage/ (BleepingComputer, published Aug 30-31, incident detected Aug 29)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / security. Frames not taken: product/dev-tooling explainer, hands-on. Angle: stakes-first — what a Claude user should actually understand and do, before the mechanism.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: stakes-first.

---

If you use Claude and your PC already has fairly ordinary malware sitting on it, someone else may have been quietly spending your subscription this week, without your password and without triggering two-factor authentication.

Anthropic disclosed on August 30 that a wave of infostealer malware, the kind that scrapes saved passwords and browser data off an infected machine, has also been stealing something more specific: active Claude login sessions. When you're logged into Claude, your browser holds a session token proving you're still signed in. Steal that token and you skip the password and the one-time code entirely, you just walk into the account as if you were already sitting in the chair.

Six malware families are behind the campaign, by Anthropic's own count: Vidar, Lumma, StealC, RedLine, and Acreed on Windows, plus Atomic Stealer on a smaller number of Macs. These are pre-existing, widely circulated tools, nothing purpose-built against Claude. Anthropic's response has been to sign affected users out, strip saved payment methods off compromised accounts, and refund charges it can identify as unauthorized.

The part worth sitting with is what this isn't. Claude's own systems weren't breached, and Anthropic has been explicit about that: "We have no reason to believe that this malware is related to Claude, installed through Claude, or related to anything you did with Claude." What it does show is how much of an AI product's identity now lives in a browser session rather than a password, and a session is only as safe as the machine underneath it. Signing someone out kills the stolen session. It does nothing about the malware still running on their laptop, which is why Anthropic is also pushing people to actually clean the infected machine, not just log back in.

## 2. OpenAI is cutting Cursor off over its new owner. Anthropic, tied to the same owner, is doing the opposite.

**Source:** https://www.forbes.com/sites/jonmarkman/2026/08/31/openai-cuts-off-cursor-after-spacexs-60-billion-takeover/ (Forbes, published Aug 31; corroborated by CNBC Aug 29 and The State of AI)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business. Frames not taken: dev-tooling product explainer, technique/pattern piece on distillation. Angle: frame-shift — this reads as "OpenAI protects its IP from Musk"; the sharper story is Anthropic holding the identical rule and not applying it.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift.

---

OpenAI just told SpaceX it's cutting off Cursor's direct access to its models on November 12. The reason has nothing to do with the product and everything to do with who bought it.

Elon Musk's SpaceX closed a $60 billion all-stock purchase of Anysphere, the company that makes the AI coding tool Cursor, on August 14. OpenAI's contract with Cursor includes a change-of-control clause, a standard term that lets a company exit when its counterparty gets bought by someone new, and OpenAI is invoking it, citing X's history of breaking contract terms after Musk bought Twitter, plus Musk's own sworn admission earlier this year that xAI had distilled OpenAI's outputs. Distillation means training a smaller model on a rival's outputs to cheaply approximate its capabilities, and every major lab's usage policy bans it. OpenAI's read: it cannot keep sending its models into a company now owned by the operator of a competing frontier lab running its own giant compute cluster.

Anthropic's usage policy bans the exact same thing, training a competing model on Claude's outputs, and Cursor is a major surface for Claude too. Instead of following OpenAI's move, Anthropic announced it would increase Claude compute for Cursor. It's also worth knowing that Anthropic has been buying compute from SpaceX since May, including capacity on SpaceX's Colossus cluster, and tied that deal directly to higher usage limits for Claude Code and API customers. None of this proves Anthropic is holding back specifically because SpaceX is now its landlord, but the two companies are applying an identical rule to the identical buyer and landing in opposite places, and the more commercially exposed one is the one that didn't cut ties.

For Cursor users, the practical damage looks small. CEO Michael Truell says OpenAI models are about 5% of the platform's traffic, and Grok, Claude, and Gemini all stay available. The interesting question isn't whether Cursor survives this, it's what a "usage policy" is actually worth once the company enforcing it is also a customer of the buyer it would need to enforce it against.

## 3. China just made a corporate fraud dispute a precondition for US-China AI talks

**Source:** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-31/china-rebukes-anthropic-sets-terms-for-key-us-china-ai-dialogue (Bloomberg, published Aug 31)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business (geopolitics). Frames not taken: AI security deep-dive on distillation attacks, governance/policy piece. Angle: frame-shift — coverage reads this as a US-China spat; the actual leverage point is a specific, still-unadjudicated corporate extraction claim.

Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / business. Angle: frame-shift.

---

The first real US-China dialogue on AI is supposed to happen soon, and on Sunday, Chinese state-affiliated media set the terms for it before a single meeting has occurred.

A post on Yuyuantantian, an account tied to Chinese state broadcaster CCTV, said the US has to prove its own AI companies follow the same safety, disclosure, and audit rules it wants applied to Chinese ones before any "substantive" talks can happen. The trigger is a specific accusation, not a general grievance. Back in June, Anthropic told US senators that operators tied to Alibaba's Qwen lab ran roughly 25,000 fake accounts to query Claude 28.8 million times between April 22 and June 5, and used the results to train Qwen. That's a distillation attack, industrial-scale querying of a rival model so you can cheaply train a smaller one to imitate it, and Anthropic called it the largest such campaign it has ever caught, nearly double what it attributed to three other Chinese labs back in February. Anthropic also alleged the Chinese government was complicit, and separately, it has blocked its own services from Chinese-controlled companies on national-security grounds.

What's easy to miss under the diplomatic framing is that the dispute at the center of this isn't really about AI safety in the treaty sense at all. It's an IP-theft accusation that neither government has independently verified in public, now being used as leverage over whether a safety dialogue happens at all. China's response doesn't rebut the 28.8 million query figure, it reframes the whole exchange as a fairness question about whose companies get scrutinized.
