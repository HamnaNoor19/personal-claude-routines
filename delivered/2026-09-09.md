## 1. OpenAI says its AI solved a 90-year-old math problem. Nobody outside OpenAI has checked yet.

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability evaluation. Frames not taken: pure industry news (there's no product here, no launch); discourse (the credit dispute is real but secondary to the technical claim). Angle: frame-shift — this is being covered as "AI solved math," the sharper story is that verification hasn't happened and there's an active credit fight underneath.

---

OpenAI says one of its unreleased models just resolved a piece of a problem that's sat open for about 90 years: whether the equations that describe how fluids move, the same equations behind weather forecasting and airplane design, can ever spontaneously break down. The model's answer is yes. It describes a scenario where a vortex spins tighter and faster forever in finite time, a mathematical event called blowup, while the fluid's total energy stays bounded the whole time.

This is a piece of one of the Clay Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, seven famous unsolved questions with a million-dollar prize sitting on each one. OpenAI ran roughly 1,000 AI agents for 50 hours on a related, easier equation first, then scaled to 10,000 agents for an 11-hour final push, and published a 166-page manuscript alongside a machine-checked formal proof on September 8. The company says it won't take the prize money.

What the announcement doesn't claim: that anyone outside OpenAI has checked this yet. The Clay Institute's own rules require journal publication and two years of community scrutiny before any prize gets awarded, and some mathematicians reading the release argue the machine-checked version covers a "forced" variant of the equations, not quite the exact statement of the original problem.

There's also a messier thread running underneath. NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster says he and Levent Alpöge, who works at Anthropic, had spent close to a year closing in on the same result before OpenAI's sprint, and that OpenAI offered him credit only if Alpöge was left off the paper. OpenAI denies using any of their unpublished work.

Both things can be true at once: a genuinely striking technical result, and a claim that isn't done being checked or credited yet.

## 2. A government advisory accuses six Chinese AI firms of stealing US models. Its fix could mean your own account gets quietly downgraded.

**Source:** https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa26-251a
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security (adversarial extraction via API access, not alignment/interpretability/evals). Angle: frame-shift — this is being covered as a US-vs-China tech-theft accusation; the part that actually reaches an ordinary paying customer is the proposed countermeasure.

---

A joint advisory from the NSA, CISA, and FBI, published September 8, makes an accusation with an odd twist. It isn't just that six China-based AI companies are alleged to have stolen from US frontier models. It's that the government's own recommended fix could mean your paid API access gets quietly swapped for a worse model, with no explanation given.

The technique at the center is called distillation: training a smaller, cheaper model by having it imitate the outputs of a bigger one. That's a completely normal, legal method AI companies use on their own models all the time. The advisory alleges six companies, DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI, did this covertly against competitors' models, running huge volumes of queries through the same public login any paying customer uses, to harvest their behavior for free instead of paying for the research.

DeepSeek gets singled out specifically. The advisory says it pulled outputs from at least eleven other models, spanning Claude 3.7 through Opus 4.1 and GPT-4 through GPT-5, since late 2024, to build training data behind its R1 and V3 models, and it directly disputes DeepSeek's widely cited $5.6 million training cost as misleading because it excludes that data's real cost. The agencies call the practice central to how these companies build models, not an occasional shortcut, and say it likely happened with the Chinese government's awareness. None of that has been tested in court or verified by anyone outside these three agencies.

Where this gets uncomfortable for ordinary users is the mitigation. The advisory's own signals for spotting a distiller, nonstop usage with no idle periods, brand-new accounts instantly pushing as much traffic as the system allows, shared logins from many locations, describe a busy startup or research lab just as well as an attacker. Its fix is for providers to quietly serve flagged accounts a cheaper, less capable model instead of cutting them off, and specifically not tell the customer why, on the theory that a warning just teaches real distillers how to blend in.

Whether the underlying accusation holds up is one question. Whether silently downgrading some paying customers is a policy worth accepting is a separate one, and it doesn't wait on the first being settled.

## 3. Google DeepMind's genome-wide prediction map just found a real diagnosis a doctor had missed for years.

**Source:** https://deepmind.google/blog/alphagenome-atlas-a-predictive-map-of-every-possible-dna-letter-change-in-the-human-genome/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation, applied to science. Frames not taken: product launch (this is a research dataset, not a developer product); industry news (no business story here). Angle: stakes-first, hands-on-emotional-core — lead with the diagnosis, let the petabyte scale support it rather than open with it.

---

A rare-disease case that had stumped doctors for years just got a diagnosis, and the tool that cracked it did so by checking a spot in the genome that conventional genetic testing routinely skips.

Researchers at the Broad Institute, working with the GREGoR Consortium (a research network focused on unsolved rare-disease genomes), were stuck on a patient with a severe seizure disorder called epileptic encephalopathy. The usual approach is to sequence the protein-coding part of the genome, the roughly 2% of DNA that directly builds proteins, and look for an obviously broken gene there. This patient didn't have one. They had a gene called DNM1, already linked to the condition, with no coding mutation to explain it.

Google DeepMind's newly released AlphaGenome Atlas flagged a single-letter change buried deep in DNM1's non-coding DNA, the kind of variant that's easy to miss because it never touches a protein directly. The model predicted this change creates a hidden splice site, a spot where the cell's machinery cuts and rejoins genetic instructions, that adds 13 extra amino acids to the resulting protein. Wet-lab experiments confirmed the prediction, and the case was reclassified as likely pathogenic. In plain terms: a real diagnosis where there had been none.

That single call sits inside something much bigger. AlphaGenome Atlas is a roughly 1-petabyte, freely available database, more than 30 times the size of DeepMind's AlphaFold protein database by the company's own comparison, that precomputes a prediction for every one of the roughly 9 billion possible single-letter changes across the human genome, plus over 100 million real insertions and deletions seen in large population studies. Each variant gets one number, the AVI score, meant to help researchers sort the changes worth investigating from the millions that aren't.

One diagnosis doesn't prove the whole atlas works as intended, and DeepMind's own framing is that this is a triage tool, not a replacement for the wet-lab work that actually closed this case. But it's a concrete example of a precomputed prediction pointing a human researcher at the one variant that mattered among billions of candidates, and that's a more useful kind of AI result than another benchmark number.
