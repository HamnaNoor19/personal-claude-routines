## 1. A solo developer shipped a 125M-parameter model that plays piano with you, entirely on your phone

**Source:** https://simedw.com/2026/08/20/midi-autocomplete/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Technique / hands-on observation — niche concept made legible: while frontier labs chase scale, a real-time, useful generative model just shipped at 125M parameters, running fully on-device.

---

Most of the AI headlines this month are about models that need a data center. This one runs entirely on an iPhone, with no internet connection, and it plays piano with you.

A developer who goes by simedw built a 125-million-parameter model, tiny by AI standards, that predicts what note you're about to play next while you're playing a real piano. Same idea as code autocomplete tools like GitHub Copilot, except the prompt is a few notes on a MIDI keyboard (a digital keyboard that sends note data, not audio) instead of a line of code. It runs fast enough to keep up with live playing: about 108 notes per second on an iPhone 15, entirely on the device, no server round-trip.

What made it work wasn't a bigger model. It was getting the representation right (a compact way of describing each note's pitch, timing, duration, and how hard it was struck), cleaning the training data aggressively, and a preference-based fine-tuning step (DPO, training a model on which of two outputs a human liked better) to keep the output musical instead of just statistically likely. The app, RollTab, is free if you own a MIDI keyboard. It hit #1 on Hacker News with 549 points.

It's a one-person project in a narrow niche, not a benchmark result, and it says nothing about whether this generalizes past music. But it's a clean, working counterexample to the assumption that useful, real-time generative AI needs a frontier-scale model and a cloud connection behind it. Sometimes it needs 125 million parameters and someone who cared about the data.

## 2. Anthropic mandated data retention to catch cyberattacks. OpenAI just used that opening.

**Source:** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/anthropic-plans-to-change-data-retention-policy-for-advanced-ai
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security — synthesis: two labs converged on pattern-based misuse detection from opposite starting points, within the same week.

---

If you send anything sensitive to Claude or ChatGPT because the vendor promises "zero data retention," this week is worth paying attention to. Both companies have just admitted, in different ways, that the promise has been in tension with something else they care about: catching AI-enabled cyberattacks.

Back in June, Anthropic quietly started retaining all customer data for 30 days on its most capable models, a reversal from zero retention, specifically to help detect new kinds of cyberattacks carried out using its models. Anthropic's own report was candid that this would be unpopular: it said the policy would "be unpopular with customers who have come to expect zero retention, and pose real risks to our business success, especially if competitors do not follow." According to Bloomberg, competitors didn't follow, and enterprise customers pushed back. Anthropic now plans to let business customers keep that mandated data on their own cloud infrastructure instead of Anthropic's, a system it says it built with feedback from more than 100 customers, including Salesforce.

Days before that reporting, OpenAI previewed its own answer to the same problem: Private Safety Processing. Rather than storing raw prompts and outputs, automated systems scan for patterns of misuse across a customer's interactions and surface only a narrow signal, the category of concern, without giving OpenAI staff access to the underlying content. It's being tested with a small group of customers now, with a wider rollout and technical write-up due in September.

Neither company has fully solved this. Anthropic's on-prem option and OpenAI's pattern-only monitoring are both previews, not yet defaults, and the real test is what actually gets flagged and how often either approach is wrong, details that aren't public yet [TODO: verify once OpenAI's September technical write-up lands]. But it's notable that two labs, starting from opposite ends (retain everything vs. retain nothing), converged on the same underlying idea within the same week: catch misuse from patterns, not from reading the actual conversation. That's a narrower and more specific claim than "AI companies care about your privacy," and it's worth tracking whether the pattern-based approach holds up once outside researchers can examine it.

## 3. Linear published two years of its own AI usage data. The number that matters isn't adoption.

**Source:** https://linear.app/data
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business (a company's own product data) — frame-shift: the adoption story everyone tells vs. the time story the data actually shows.

---

Linear, the project-tracking tool a lot of engineering teams run on, just published two years of its own usage data, and the number that stands out isn't the adoption curve. It's what happened to time.

Adoption climbed fast. Two years ago, fewer than 1 in 1,000 issues created in Linear were written by AI. Now it's just under half, and at the current rate AI will soon author more issues than people and integrations combined. Teams that connected a coding agent roughly tripled their weekly pull requests (batches of code changes submitted for review), from 21 to 65, over two years. Teams without one went from 8 to 10.

What undercuts the usual pitch is time spent on existing tasks in Linear: it held roughly flat. AI didn't replace hours of work, it added a new layer of work (reviewing, prompting, steering agents) on top of what was already there. Total time spent on product development went up, not down.

None of this is an independent audit; it's Linear measuring its own paid workspaces, and it only sees what happens inside Linear, not the rest of an engineer's day. But it's a real company's own data contradicting its own industry's favorite claim, that AI coding tools are primarily a time-saving story. The more honest framing, at least by this data, is that they're a volume-increasing story, and volume and time saved are not the same thing.
