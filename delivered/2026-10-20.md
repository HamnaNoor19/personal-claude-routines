## 1. Claude designed proteins that bind scorpion toxins. 17 of 211 worked in the lab.
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-scorpion-toxin-binder-design
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Capability evaluation, stakes-first with skeptical honesty about the hit rate and what wasn't tested.

Scorpion stings kill more than 3,250 people a year, mostly children. Anthropic just published what happens when you hand that problem to an AI model and mostly get out of the way.

An internal Claude model was asked to design small proteins that latch onto five scorpion neurotoxins (the venom molecules that do the damage), as a first step toward cheaper antivenom. Over 64 hours it generated 318,484 candidate designs using 12 design tools, screened about 553,000 design-toxin pairs on the computer, and picked 180 to be built. It chose the toxin structures, set its own quality bars, and emailed the sequences to the DNA synthesis company.

The honest scoreboard: 17 of 211 synthesized designs (about 8%) bound a toxin tightly in tests at two separate labs. For designs aimed at a single toxin it was 11 of 60, which Anthropic says is typical of human-run campaigns. The two best bound at single-digit nanomolar strength. The designs meant to hit all five toxins at once? Zero worked for more than one.

I'd resist reading this as "AI cured scorpion stings." Nobody tested whether these proteins neutralize venom in cells or animals, and the tests used lab-made toxin, not real venom. What it does show is that one model can run a design-build-test loop for about 24 hours before a human steps in. My open question is how a result like this gets reviewed when it's released with all 211 sequences, since the post doesn't discuss dual-use.

**Frame / angle:** Interpretability. Contested claim, describe positions without adjudicating, stakes-first.

If you can read what is happening inside an AI model, you can check it for deception instead of trusting what it says. That is the whole bet behind interpretability research, and a viral essay this week argues the bet may be easier to win than the field assumed.

The author is Nick Cammarata of Goodfire, an interpretability company. His claim, as I understand it from coverage (I couldn't open the essay itself): the famous "tangled neurons" problem, where one internal unit seems to respond to unrelated things, was mostly a misreading. His example is a 2020 image model neuron that fired for both cars and cats, which he reads as responding to a shared V shape. A preliminary audit of Qwen3-32B reportedly found roughly 90% of neurons have one main job [TODO: verify figure and thresholds], versus under 2% judged interpretable in OpenAI's earlier automated attempt.

I find the car-and-cat reading appealing, and I also can't evaluate it. By the reports, there is no peer review, no external replication, and the technical paper is still weeks away. Stanford's Chris Potts is quoted calling it little more than a hunch, and Jacob Hilton argues researchers understand far less than the essay implies [TODO: verify quotes].

So the narrow thing this shows is one researcher's reanalysis plus a preliminary audit on one model. Whether it extends to the models that matter for safety is a separate, open question. I'll be waiting for the companion paper.

## 3. Anthropic commits 5% of its shares to a new Public Benefit Fund
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/public-benefit-fund
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news, skeptical read: big headline number, governance still blank.

Anthropic announced on Oct 18 that it will put 5% of its shares into a new Public Benefit Fund, and says that with earlier pledges roughly 20% of the company is now earmarked for public good. That is a very large number for a company of this size, so I read the fine print.

What's actually committed: 5% of shares now, subject to stockholder approval, plus up to 2% more at the board's discretion each time the company's value rises 50% above a baseline Anthropic hasn't disclosed. The Fund is a wholly-owned LLC, and the equity it holds can't be used for lobbying or political giving. The stated uses include AI safety research, biomedical research, discounted Claude access for nonprofits, and cushioning labor-market disruption.

The "roughly 20%" is a sum: the 5%, the co-founders' pledge to give away 80% of their own equity, and an employee match program. Those are different instruments with different owners. The post gives no dollar value, and says structure, governance and milestones will come "in the coming months."

My read: the lobbying restriction is a concrete, checkable commitment, and the rest is an intention until someone sees who controls the Fund and what it can sell. If you want a one-line test for whether this matters, it's whether the governance document ever lands.
