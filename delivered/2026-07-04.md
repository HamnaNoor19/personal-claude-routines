## 1. OpenAI's GeneBench-Pro: frontier models still fail real biology judgment 7 times out of 10

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/introducing-genebench-pro/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation — a number that lands without a glossary, leading with the failure rate instead of the win.

OpenAI put out a biology benchmark this week where the best model available still gets the answer wrong about 7 times out of 10. That's the headline, and I think it's more useful than a benchmark where someone announces a new high score.

The benchmark is called GeneBench-Pro. It's 129 problems spread across ten areas of genetics research (cancer genomics, pharmacogenomics, forensic genetics, and others), and each one is built with a known correct answer baked in so graders can check not just whether a model got the right conclusion, but whether it got there for the right reason. That second part is the actual point. Most AI benchmarks test recall: does the model know the fact. This one hands the model a messy, realistic dataset, the kind with errors and ambiguity a real researcher would have to notice, and asks it to figure out what's wrong, pick a sound analysis approach, and land on a conclusion someone could actually act on.

The scores: OpenAI's own GPT-5.6 Sol Pro, run at its highest reasoning setting, passed 31.5% of problems. Its faster sibling, GPT-5.6 Sol, got 28.7%. Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.8 was the best non-OpenAI model tested and passed 16.0%.

Here's the number that actually stopped me: reviewers estimate a single one of these problems would take a trained researcher 20 to 40 hours to work through properly. Running it through one of these models costs a few dollars. So even at a 30% success rate, you're looking at a tool that's wrong most of the time but, when it's right, replaces a week of expert labor for pocket change.

What this shows: current frontier models are genuinely bad at open-ended scientific judgment, not just occasionally wrong at the margins. What it doesn't show: which 30% they get right, or whether a researcher without genomics training could tell the difference between a good answer and a confident-sounding bad one. That gap is exactly where I'd worry about people trusting output they can't independently check.

## 2. Meituan's LongCat-2.0: the chip story matters more than the open-source claim

**Source:** https://www.longcatai.org/models/longcat-2
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation — frame-shift: coverage is calling this an open-source release, but the weights aren't out, and the more consequential fact is what chips it was trained on.

The headline going around is that Meituan open-sourced a trillion-parameter AI model. Read the fine print and the more interesting story is what it was trained on, not what license is stamped on it.

The model is LongCat-2.0, announced June 30. It's a mixture-of-experts model (a design where the model has many specialized sub-networks and only activates a relevant slice of them per query, so most of its size doesn't show up in the compute cost of a single answer) with 1.6 trillion parameters total but only about 48 billion of them active for any given token. It has a native 1-million-token context window, and on coding benchmarks it's genuinely competitive: 59.5 on SWE-bench Pro versus GPT-5.5's 58.6, though it still trails Claude Opus 4.8 on broader agent tasks.

Here's the part I'd lead with if I were writing the headline: Meituan trained and served this thing end to end, pretraining through inference, on a cluster of more than 50,000 domestic Chinese AI chips, with zero Nvidia or Google TPU hardware anywhere in the stack. If that holds up, it's the first trillion-parameter-class model built entirely outside the hardware that export controls were designed to keep out of China's hands. That's the actual news. A benchmark score two points above GPT-5.5 is a Tuesday; training at this scale on chips nobody expected to be ready yet is the thing worth updating on.

Now the caveat, and it matters: the "open source" framing is ahead of the facts. Meituan registered the repo under an MIT license, which is about as permissive as licenses get, but as of this week the actual weights aren't there. Both the GitHub repo and the Hugging Face model card say "coming soon." Anyone citing this as a working open-weight model right now is citing a promise, not a download.

So the honest version is two claims, not one: the chip independence looks real and important, and the openness is a license waiting on a file that hasn't landed yet. Worth watching whether it does.

## 3. Unsealed court docs: the Pentagon official pushing Anthropic on autonomous weapons cashed out a rival AI stake mid-dispute

**Source:** https://www.techtimes.com/articles/319713/20260704/pentagon-blacklisted-anthropic-over-autonomous-weapons-limits-emails-reveal-very-close-talks.htm
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Governance / policy — a number that lands without a glossary: the stock sale, not the blacklisting itself, is the new fact worth sitting with.

Court documents unsealed this week hand you a strange detail: the Pentagon official pushing hardest for an AI company to drop its refusal on autonomous weapons had, around the same time, sold his stake in a rival AI company for up to 48 times what he'd disclosed owning.

Some background, because the shape of the dispute matters before the new fact does. The Department of Defense told Anthropic it wanted to use Claude for "all lawful purposes." Anthropic was willing to work with DoD broadly, but drew a line at two specific uses: fully autonomous weapons systems and domestic mass surveillance. When Anthropic held that line past a deadline, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth designated the company a "supply chain risk," a label usually reserved for foreign adversaries, and told defense contractors to stop using its AI. Anthropic challenged this in court and lost an appeals bid to block it back in April.

Here's what's new. Documents unsealed July 2 show a private email the day after that blacklisting, in which Emil Michael, the Pentagon's Under Secretary of Defense for Research and Engineering and its lead negotiator on this deal, told Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei that talks were "very close." So the same week Anthropic got publicly labeled a security risk, the government's own negotiator was privately signaling the two sides were nearly there.

The financial detail: Office of Government Ethics filings show Michael held xAI stock, Elon Musk's competing AI company, worth $500,000 to $1 million at his original disclosure. He sold it on January 9 for somewhere between $5 million and $25 million, a return of 400 to 4,800 percent.

What's confirmed: the blacklisting, the "very close" email, and the stock sale figures, all from the unsealed filings and OGE records. What isn't confirmed is causation. Nothing in these documents shows the stock holding drove the pressure campaign. What's established is narrower and still worth sitting with: the official pressing hardest for a rival's product to inherit ground an AI company gave up on weapons use held a large financial stake in that rival while it happened.
