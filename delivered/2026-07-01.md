When Anthropic pulled Claude Fable 5 offline for every user on the planet on June 12, it wasn't because someone found a way to build a bioweapon with it. A security researcher at Amazon got the model to review a codebase under a "defensive security check" framing, and it flagged a few already publicly known vulnerabilities. That's the whole incident: a jailbreak, getting a model to ignore the safety training meant to block a request like that. But a U.S. export control order applied to foreign nationals everywhere, and Anthropic had no way to check user nationality in real time, so it shut the model down globally rather than risk violating the order. Eighteen days, every user worldwide, over one mid-tier jailbreak.

The model came back yesterday. The part I think matters more is what got announced next to it: Anthropic, Amazon, Microsoft, and Google are drafting a shared scale for how bad a jailbreak actually is, modeled on CVSS, the system security teams have used since 2005 to triage software vulnerabilities. Four axes: what new capability the jailbreak actually unlocks, how many people are exposed if it's exploited, how easy it is to reproduce, and how likely someone else stumbles onto it independently. A jailbreak that surfaces something a web search would already tell you scores near zero. One that helps synthesize a bioweapon precursor scores at the ceiling.

Software security has had this vocabulary for two decades. AI security has been improvising, and the default response scales to "turn it off for the whole planet" because nobody has a cheap way to say how serious an incident actually is relative to the last one. A shared severity scale won't stop jailbreaks from happening. What it might do is stop every jailbreak from triggering the same blunt, worldwide response no matter how serious it turns out to be.

Worth being honest about the catch: for now this is labs scoring their own homework, with no outside auditor. Whether the number means anything depends on whether it holds up the next time this happens for real.

## 2. Meituan open-sources LongCat-2.0 — a 1.6T-parameter model trained on zero export-controlled chips

**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter

For the last few years, the working assumption behind U.S. chip export controls has been simple: restrict the best AI hardware, and China's models stay a step behind. Meituan just open-sourced something that complicates that story.

LongCat-2.0 is a 1.6 trillion parameter model, trained and run entirely on a domestic cluster of 50,000 Huawei chips — no export-restricted hardware anywhere in the pipeline. It's a mixture-of-experts model, meaning it doesn't activate all 1.6 trillion parameters for every request. It only switches on a relevant slice, somewhere between 33 and 56 billion parameters per token, which is what makes a model this size affordable to actually run.

On the numbers Meituan has published, it's not the best coding model available. On SWE-bench Pro, a benchmark that grades how well a model handles real software engineering tasks, LongCat-2.0 scored 59.5, ahead of GPT-5.5's 58.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro's 54.2, but behind Claude Sonnet 5's 63.2. That's the honest read: competitive with the second tier of frontier models, not ahead of the frontier itself.

The ranking isn't what I'd focus on. What stands out to me is that "competitive with GPT-5.5, built on zero restricted chips, and free to download" is now a real category instead of a hypothetical one. Export controls can slow down who gets the newest hardware. They don't appear to stop a well-resourced lab from getting to "good enough to matter" on domestic silicon instead.

Worth holding two things at once: the benchmark numbers are self-reported, and open weights don't tell you what's in the training data. Both are true, and neither changes the headline number: 50,000 chips, zero of them export-controlled.

## 3. Claude Science launches — an AI agent built to show its work

**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-science-ai-workbench
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Product launch — concept made legible: reproducibility crisis in science, framed as a paper-trail problem an auditable AI agent is designed to solve.
**Verify:** Same WebFetch outage as above — pricing/eligibility details sourced from MIT Technology Review and Anthropic's own press coverage, not the primary post directly. [TODO: verify]

---

Science has a reproducibility problem that gets worse every year: a huge share of published results can't be reproduced, often because nobody kept a clean, honest record of exactly what was done to get them. Anthropic's newest product is a bet that an AI agent, if you force it to log everything, can supply the paper trail that's been missing.

Claude Science, launched this week, is positioned as "Claude Code for research" — same idea as the coding agent, aimed at lab work instead of software. It comes with more than 60 pre-built skills for specific scientific domains: genomics, proteomics, structural biology, cheminformatics, and others, so a researcher isn't starting from a blank prompt. The feature that actually interests me is the auditable-artifact claim: every figure or result the agent produces is supposed to carry a traceable history of how it got there, in principle letting someone check the work rather than just trust the output.

It's live now, in beta, to every paid Claude subscriber, not gated to enterprise customers the way a lot of "AI for science" tooling has been. Anthropic is also funding up to 50 outside research projects with up to $30,000 in credits each, with applications open through July 15.

I'd hold one honest caveat next to the pitch. Anthropic is reportedly building out product lines like this partly to diversify revenue ahead of a rumored IPO, and that context doesn't make the tool worse, but it's worth knowing the launch isn't happening in a vacuum. The other open question is whether "auditable" holds up once actual labs push on it. A traceable history is only as good as what it's tracing, and no outside group has stress-tested that yet.

Still, if it works as described, "the agent has to show its work" is a more interesting design constraint than most AI product launches bother with.
