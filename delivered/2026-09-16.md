## 1. Anthropic called it a 25% increase. Compared to what you had yesterday, it's a 17% cut.

**Source:** https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/artificial-intelligence/anthropic-is-cutting-claude-codes-current-weekly-limits-by-17-percent/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: industry news / business (a pricing-and-limits policy change, not a launch). Frames not taken: product launch (no new capability shipped), technique or pattern (not about how Claude Code works, about what you're allowed to run). Angle: frame-shift — this is being announced as a raise; measured against what users actually had access to this week, it's a reduction.

---

If you use Claude Code and felt like this week's "25% increase" announcement didn't match what showed up in your account, you read it right.

Since May, Anthropic had quietly been running Claude Code's weekly usage limits 50% above the standard level, a temporary boost through the highest-traffic months. That boost expired Sunday night. What replaced it this week was a "permanent 25% increase" to the standard limit, announced like a gift.

Read against the boosted numbers people had been building workflows around, it isn't one. On a base of 100 units a week: the boost gave you 150, the new permanent level gives you 125. That's 17% less capacity than what worked a few days ago, even though it's still 25% more than the original pre-boost baseline from before May. Anthropic later confirmed the math itself, stating the change "works out to a 17% reduction in weekly limits on Claude Code" compared to where things stood the day before.

The backlash on X was fast enough that someone attached a Community Note to Anthropic's own announcement post before the day was out.

None of this means Claude Code got worse than it was in April. It means the number in the headline and the number in your weekly quota are answering two different questions, and only one of them is the one you'll notice when you hit your limit on a Thursday.

## 2. Google just shipped the top-ranked voice AI model and priced it a third of what the model it dethroned costs

**Source:** https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-3-8-live-gemini-3-8-live-extended-thinking/ [TODO: verify — official blog wasn't directly reachable this session; details cross-checked across 5 independent outlets]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: model release / capability evaluation. Frames not taken: product launch/dev tooling (secondary here — the model itself is the story, not the integration path), industry news/business (the pricing angle matters but isn't the headline on its own). Angle: a number that lands without a glossary — lead with the price-and-rank combination, then complicate it.

---

Google DeepMind just shipped a voice AI model that's both the best-scoring option on an independent leaderboard and roughly a third of the price of the model it dethroned.

Gemini 3.8 Live and a heavier sibling, Gemini 3.8 Live Extended Thinking, went live this week in the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. These aren't chat models with a voice bolted on. They're built for real-time back-and-forth conversation, the kind a voice agent needs to hold a phone call without awkward pauses or a lag that makes it feel like a bad phone line.

Artificial Analysis, a group that benchmarks AI models independently of the labs that build them, put Extended Thinking at the top of its Speech-to-Speech Quality Index with a score of 82.6, ahead of OpenAI's GPT-Live-1 and xAI's Grok Voice. What makes that more than a leaderboard flex is the price: standard Gemini 3.8 Live runs about $0.84 an hour of audio, which Google is calling the cheapest commercial real-time speech model available, and even the top-tier Extended Thinking version costs roughly $3.50 an hour, about a third of what OpenAI charges for its comparable realtime API.

Worth some caution before reading this as a clean sweep. That 82.6 is a composite blended from several sub-scores, and on at least one harder, more realistic test, an agent completing a banking-style voice task, Extended Thinking only succeeded 35.1% of the time. A model can top a general leaderboard and still stumble on the specific job you actually need it to do.

Still, "best and cheapest" is a rare combination in this market, and it didn't hold for very long the last time someone claimed it either.

## 3. A leading AI-interpretability tool can be made to tell a different story about the same behavior, with almost no provocation

**Source:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.15533
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: interpretability. Frames not taken: alignment research (the paper is about the auditing tool's reliability, not the model's own alignment), governance/policy (no policy content), capability evals (this measures explanation reliability, not capability). Angle: niche concept made legible / frame-shift — most people treat "the model's interpretable features lit up" as itself reassuring; this paper shows that step can flip under a change that shouldn't matter at all.

---

A tool safety researchers use to check what's actually happening inside a language model can be made to tell a different story about the same behavior, just by nudging the input in a way that shouldn't matter.

One popular way to make a model's internals legible to a person is to train a second, simpler network alongside it, built out of features that light up in patterns a human can read, meant to stand in faithfully for what the real model is doing. Researchers call this an interpretable replacement network, or IRN. A new paper, led by Tobias Ladner, tests how solid that stand-in actually is.

The authors ran the same test across five different open-weight model families: GPT-2, two sizes of Gemma, Llama 3.2, and R1-Distill-Qwen. They took inputs and changed them in ways that shouldn't affect their meaning, then checked which features the IRN flagged as driving the model's output. In all five families, that small, meaning-preserving change was enough to flip which features got credited for the result. The human-readable explanation changed even though the underlying task didn't. Where the paper gets more useful is what happens next: a formal method, borrowed from techniques used to verify safety-critical software, calculates a mathematically guaranteed upper bound on how wrong an IRN's explanation could get under adversarial pressure. Left to ordinary training, that bound is loose at every layer the authors tested. Training the network with that bound as an explicit target tightens it substantially, producing explanations the authors argue a safety auditor could actually lean on.

None of this says interpretability research doesn't work. It says one widely used method's explanations aren't reliable by default on the model families tested, and that reliability has to be trained for on purpose rather than assumed.
