**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter

---

If you've hit a usage limit on Claude recently, this is part of why: Anthropic is reportedly in talks to lease up to $10 billion of computing power from Meta over the next two years.

The New York Times reported the talks on July 17, since confirmed by CNBC, Bloomberg, and CNN: Anthropic would pay Meta in monthly installments over a two-year term for compute capacity, though both sides describe it as early and say it could still fall apart. What's notable isn't that Meta wants into the AI-infrastructure-leasing business (Mark Zuckerberg said as much back in May). It's that this is Anthropic's second deal like this in about ten weeks. In May, Anthropic locked in $1.25 billion a month, running through 2029, for the entire Colossus 1 data center in Memphis, a facility with more than 220,000 Nvidia GPUs that xAI had reportedly been running at just 11% utilization.

Two multibillion-dollar compute contracts inside three months, and Anthropic is still rationing its most capable model for the people paying for it. Anthropic is also reportedly lining up investor meetings ahead of a possible IPO later this year. Locking down two independent, long-term compute suppliers before a roadshow starts is a different pitch than walking in with one supplier and a capacity ceiling. ## 2. OpenAI built an AI whose only job is attacking OpenAI's own models — and it beat human red-teamers 84% to 13% on prompt injection

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/unlocking-self-improvement-gpt-red/ (cross-referenced: MarkTechPost, Help Net Security, MIT Technology Review, cybersecuritynews.com)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI security — niche concept made legible: what prompt injection is and what a self-play red-teaming AI actually demonstrates versus implies. *Note: OpenAI published this July 15, three days before this digest's normal 24-hour window — included because nothing fresher in AI security cleared the bar this run (see rejected list).*

---

If an AI agent reads webpages or emails for you, prompt injection (hidden text that hijacks it) is the attack security people worry about most. OpenAI built an AI to find those holes first.

The system is called GPT-Red, and OpenAI described it in a blog post on July 15. It works by attacking OpenAI's own models: it sends a prompt, watches how the model responds, and adapts, the same loop a human red-teamer runs, except it does this across thousands of scenarios at once. Training uses self-play reinforcement learning, meaning GPT-Red and a set of separate "defender" models are trained against each other simultaneously. GPT-Red is rewarded for a successful attack. The defenders are rewarded for resisting it without breaking on the task they were actually asked to do.

The number that stood out to me: OpenAI ran GPT-Red against an independent benchmark built by the UK AI Security Institute and several outside labs, on scenarios GPT-Red had never trained on, targeting an older model, GPT-5.1. It succeeded on 84% of them. Human red-teamers working the same benchmark had succeeded on 13%. Feeding what GPT-Red found back into training GPT-5.6 Sol cut its failure rate against GPT-Red's hardest attacks sixfold, down to 0.05%. One attack GPT-Red found on its own, nicknamed "Fake Chain-of-Thought," plants a spoofed entry into the model's own reasoning trace so it treats an attacker's instructions as if they were its own prior thinking [TODO: verify this mechanism description against OpenAI's original writeup; sourced from secondary technical coverage since direct WebFetch to openai.com returned a 403 this session].

Worth being precise about what this shows versus what it doesn't. It's evidence a defense held up against a specific attack class, on one benchmark, against one older model, not that prompt injection is solved in general. OpenAI is upfront about the gaps: multi-turn conversations and image-based attacks still need human red-teamers, and GPT-Red itself isn't being released, specifically so the attack skill it built up doesn't end up in the wrong hands.

An AI built on purpose to be adversarial toward other AI is a stranger idea than most of what shipped this month, and probably a more consequential one for anyone building agents that touch the open web.

## 3. Anthropic calls Fable 5 "permanent" in subscriptions. The fine print is a downgrade, not a restoration.

**Source:** https://www.techtimes.com/articles/320905/20260718/claude-fable-5-ends-subscription-limbo-permanent-max-credits-only-pro.htm (cross-referenced: BleepingComputer, The Decoder, Android Authority)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift correction: "permanent" is being read as good news; the attached number says otherwise.

---

Anthropic is calling it Fable 5 becoming a "permanent" part of Claude subscriptions. The number attached to that word is worth reading before you celebrate.

Starting July 20, Claude Fable 5 (Anthropic's most capable model) is included in Max and Team Premium plans for good, according to Anthropic's own update as reported by TechTimes and BleepingComputer. That sounds like the end of a saga that started when Anthropic first threatened to pull Fable 5 out of subscriptions entirely, then extended the deadline once, then extended it again, citing demand it called hard to predict.

What "permanent" leaves out: access lands at 50% of your weekly limit, and that limit is itself smaller than it used to be. Anthropic cut Claude Code's weekly rate limits by 50% back on July 7 as a temporary measure, and that temporary boost expires the same day Fable 5 becomes "permanent." That means the 50% Fable 5 allowance is 50% of an already-reduced number, not 50% of what subscribers had a few weeks ago [TODO: verify exact baseline Anthropic is measuring against; sourced from TechTimes and secondary reporting, not Anthropic's own pricing page]. Pro and Team Standard subscribers get a one-time $100 credit, then fall to API pricing at $10 to $50 per million tokens depending on plan.

None of this happens in isolation. The same week, Anthropic was reportedly in early talks to lease up to $10 billion in computing power from Meta, on top of the $1.25-billion-a-month deal with SpaceX signed in May [TODO: verify whether Anthropic has directly tied these compute deals to the Fable 5 limits; that link is my inference from the timing, not a confirmed Anthropic statement]. A company buying up GPUs at that scale while still rationing its flagship model isn't necessarily bad news. It likely means demand outpaced even aggressive capacity planning, though it also means "permanent" is doing more marketing work than engineering work in this announcement.

If you're paying for Max or Team Premium hoping for full access back, July 20 gets you closer, not all the way.
