## 1. Tidal says 43% of the music arriving on its platform is wholly machine-made, and starts deleting it December 9
**Source:** Tidal announcement, Nov 24, 2026, via Tidal's own post ("Tidal Is Deleting, Rejecting, and Banning Fully AI-Generated Music", https://x.com/TIDAL/article/2125233598714487170) and coverage including https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/24/tidal-to-ban-and-remove-millions-of-ai-generated-tracks-as-ai-slop-inundates-music-streaming/ , https://www.rollingstone.com/music/music-news/tidal-bans-ai-music-1235663794/ , https://variety.com/2026/music/news/tidal-bans-ai-generated-music-tracks-1236956924/ (none reachable from this session; figures and Tidal's wording via search excerpts) [TODO: verify numbers against Tidal's own post]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news. Not taken: model release, hands-on observation. Angle: frame-shift. The story is read as "streamer bans AI music"; the actual move is a platform paying a vendor to adjudicate "fully generated" vs "assisted" on millions of files, with royalties on the other side of the call.

Over the last 90 days, 43% of all music delivered to Tidal was detected as entirely machine-generated. Starting December 9, the platform begins deleting it.

Tidal announced this on Nov 24. By its own count, roughly 27 million tracks already in the catalog are tagged as wholly AI-generated, about 6.3% of everything available on the service. Distributors will also be blocked from delivering new ones, and uploads whose metadata says a track is the sole output of an AI tool get rejected at the door. Detection runs on technology from Ircam Amplify, a partner Tidal had already been using to label and demonetize this material.

The part worth sitting with is where the line falls. Tidal is not banning AI from music. Tracks made with AI assistance stay, and only tracks it judges to be 100% generated go. That distinction now has to be drawn by an automated detector, at the scale of millions of files, with payouts attached to the verdict. A false positive is a working musician who used a generation tool for one layer and gets pulled anyway. I have not seen published accuracy or appeals numbers for the detector, which is the figure I would most want before calling this clean.

To be fair about scale, Tidal is a small streamer next to Spotify or Apple Music, so this is a precedent more than a market event. Reports also disagree on the eventual total, with some saying over 29 million tracks removed by the start of 2027.

For anyone shipping generated media, the practical takeaway is narrower than the headline: "was a human involved, and how much" is turning into a metadata field with money riding on it.

## 2. DeepMind's research lead says agents now hold part of the lab's compute budget, and the lab is deliberately not training their reasoning
**Source:** Koray Kavukcuoglu (Google DeepMind research VP) on DeepMind's own podcast, reported Nov 25, 2026; primary coverage https://the-decoder.com/ai-agents-now-control-part-of-deepminds-compute-for-gemini-experiments-but-recursive-self-improvement-remains-distant/ (not reachable from this session; details via search excerpts and secondary mentions such as https://zerohour.day/item/6bac70b0e1802a36b52579d953e789668132cb7b and https://pivotnews.ai/five/deepmind-gives-ai-agents-compute-to-run-gemini-experiments) [TODO: verify against the podcast episode itself — no primary transcript was reachable]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / oversight (not interpretability, not evals). Angle: stakes-first. The headline is "agents run Gemini experiments"; the load-bearing detail is the design choice made to keep watching them.

AI agents now control part of Google DeepMind's computing budget and run experiments for upcoming Gemini models without a human picking every setting. That's from the lab's own research lead, on the lab's own podcast, reported Nov 25.

The sentence that stopped me was not that one. It was the reason given for a specific restraint: DeepMind says it does not reward the agents' step-by-step written reasoning during training, so that the reasoning stays readable in plain language before the agent acts.

That is a real bet, and a fragile one. If you train a system on how its reasoning looks, you get reasoning that looks good. Leave it untrained and you keep a window into what the thing is about to do, at some cost in polish. Choosing the window over the polish, at a lab that is handing agents real compute, is the most informative part of the story.

What this actually shows is narrow: one senior researcher describing one lab's current internal setup, with no figure given for what share of compute the agents hold. Kavukcuoglu also said the lab remains far from recursive self-improvement, meaning a closed loop where models upgrade themselves without people in it. I would not treat agents scheduling training runs as evidence that loop is forming. It is a workflow change with an oversight question attached.

My read, and I might be wrong: the useful thing to track from here is not how much compute the agents get. It is whether the no-reward-on-reasoning rule survives the first time it costs the lab performance.

## 3. The FBI says attacks run with one openly published AI security tool reached privileged access in under three hours. This week it surfaced in reporting on US federal breaches
**Source:** FBI FLASH advisory on ARTEX (CSA 2026-261113), dated Nov 13, 2026, via coverage including https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/fbi-warns-artex-ai-hacking-tool-is-powering-global-cyberattacks/ , https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/fbi-growing-abuse-ai-pentesting/ and https://www.forbes.com/sites/larsdaniel/2026/11/24/fbi-warns-hackers-are-using-artex-ai-tool-to-automate-cyberattacks/ ; this week's incidents via https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/24/politics/cia-odni-hack-unclassified-information , https://therecord.media/treasury-fincen-takes-systems-offline-after-cyber-incident and https://federalnewsnetwork.com/cybersecurity/2026/11/treasury-fincen-systems-latest-to-be-hit-by-cyber-incident/ (none reachable from this session; details via search excerpts) [TODO: verify the three-hour figure and sector list against the FBI advisory text]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security (explicitly not alignment; the straddle is dual-use release norms, named as such). Angle: stakes-first with a hard shows-vs-implies split. Resisted the "AI hacked the CIA" framing the coverage invites.

Some intrusions run with ARTEX went from first contact to privileged access in under three hours, according to an FBI advisory this month. ARTEX is not malware. It is an open-source penetration testing framework, the kind of tool defenders use to attack their own systems, with AI agents carrying out the steps from a plain-language goal.

Two things landed this week. On Nov 24, reporting described an intrusion into an unclassified open-source intelligence system used by the CIA, ODNI and NGA, with sources believing ARTEX was involved. Hours later, Treasury's financial crimes bureau took systems offline after unauthorized access and pushed filing deadlines to Dec 30. Officials have not publicly linked the two.

Keep the confirmed and the inferred apart, because the coverage does not. Confirmed: the FBI issued a FLASH advisory on Nov 13 about criminal and state-aligned abuse of this framework, the developer pulled the public repository in early October after abuse reports, and forks and a paid edition keep circulating. Reported but unconfirmed: that ARTEX was the tool used against those agencies, and that China was behind it. Both rest on anonymous sourcing. A shared tool identifies nobody.

The part I keep turning over is the release question, which is the one place this touches the broader safety argument about open capability. A tool built for defenders, published openly, with agents doing the chaining, measurably compressed attacker timelines against well-resourced targets. Pulling the repository in October did not take the capability back. That is the dual-use problem in its least theoretical form yet, and it arrived through security tooling rather than through a frontier model release.
