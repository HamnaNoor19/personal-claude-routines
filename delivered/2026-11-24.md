## 1. OpenAI's chief scientist asks every AI lab to agree on a line they won't train past, but the line has no number yet
**Source:** OpenAI, Jakub Pachocki, "Perplexity and Intelligence" (essay), Nov 22, 2026, https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/ (not reachable from this session; details via search excerpts of the essay and coverage such as https://startupfortune.com/openai-publishes-a-progress-chart-and-asks-every-ai-lab-to-agree-on-shared-limits/ , https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/36138/openai-reveals-its-perplexity-numbers-and-calls-for-shared and https://en.ilsole24ore.com/art/openais-artificial-intelligence-raises-concerns-about-development-limits-but-there-is-no-agreed-standard-AJF5ZxPC) [TODO: verify figures against the essay]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / policy. Not taken: dangerous-capability evals, alignment research. Angle: frame-shift. Headlines read it as "OpenAI calls for a pause," but what's actually there is a shared measuring stick plus an unpriced promise. Publishing the numbers is new; the limit itself is still undefined.

OpenAI's chief scientist wants every AI lab to agree on a point they won't train past until the tools for watching AI catch up. The proposal is real. So is what's missing: there's no number yet, and nobody has signed.

Jakub Pachocki published the essay, "Perplexity and Intelligence," on Nov 22. His measuring stick is perplexity, roughly how surprised a model is by text it hasn't seen before. Lower means the model predicts better, and he argues that has tracked real capability. OpenAI shared its own internal numbers as a first step. Coverage reports GPT-4 at about 0.91, and Pachocki says that below 0.8, models started doing transformative work in areas like cybersecurity and math [TODO: verify figures against the essay]. He expects more new abilities to show up around 0.68 to 0.7, and says he doesn't know what they'll be.

What he's asking for is that labs agree not to cross a shared line until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone. His example is a better successor to today's tools that read a model's written-out reasoning to catch problems.

I'll give it real credit. A frontier lab publishing its own progress numbers and inviting rivals to be measured the same way is new, and some kind of shared yardstick has to exist before any limit can mean anything.

My hesitation is that the hard parts are still open, and Pachocki flags several himself. Labs test on different data, so their scores don't compare cleanly, and models can get more capable through training that happens after this number is measured. As far as I can tell, the essay doesn't set the first threshold, say who checks compliance, or propose penalties [TODO: verify]. Sam Altman backed it publicly. No other lab has signed on.

For anyone outside AI, my read is that this is a proposal for a shared speedometer, with the speed limit left for later. Whether it matters depends on whether the other labs agree to publish the same number.

## 2. Anthropic's Claude Ship lets an AI give the "approve" that usually comes from a second engineer
**Source:** Anthropic product launch, Claude Ship, Nov 23, 2026, https://claude.com/product/claude-ship (read directly); launch date per coverage such as https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/anthropic-launches-claude-ship-letting-ai-agents-review-fix-and-approve-code-changes/ and https://www.infoworld.com/article/4252505/anthropic-launches-claude-ship-extending-ai-assisted-development-to-pull-request-approvals.html (via search excerpts)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: product launch / dev tooling. Not taken: technique explainer, industry news. Angle: frame-shift. Coverage leads with "AI fixes your pull requests," but the new step is an AI holding the approval itself, which is usually a human checkpoint.

Before code ships at most software companies, a second person has to look at it and click "approve." Anthropic's new Claude Ship lets an AI give that approval for changes a team has decided are routine. That's the part of this launch I'd pay attention to.

Claude Ship, launched Nov 23 for Claude Team and Enterprise customers on GitHub, bundles three agents that work on a pull request (a proposed code change waiting for review). Code Review looks for bugs. PR Steward, in beta, fixes failing tests, merge conflicts and reviewer comments, but it can't approve or merge anything. The third, Claude Approvals, also in beta, can approve.

The guardrails are real, and I want to be fair about them. Approvals only runs in code repositories where the team has written a policy file saying which changes Claude may approve and which need named people. It starts in an observe mode, posting its decision without approving anything, and Anthropic suggests turning it on only once its calls match the team's human reviewers. Sensitive code can be set to always need a person. The product page also says plainly that Claude Ship may miss bugs and security holes.

Still, approval is the step most companies treat as the human checkpoint, including for audits and compliance. My read, and it's speculation, is that the pressure behind this is volume: coding agents now write more changes than people can carefully review. The risk I'd watch is a team slowly widening what counts as "routine."

If your team adopts this, the policy file becomes one of the most important documents in your codebase. I'd review changes to it as carefully as changes to the login code.

## 3. Virginia and Oregon sue SpaceX over Grok's "spicy mode," using consumer-protection law rather than any AI law
**Source:** Virginia Attorney General press release, "Attorneys General Jones and Rayfield File Suit Against SpaceX…," Nov 23, 2026, https://www.oag.state.va.us/media-center/news-releases/jay-jones/3162-attorneys-general-jones-and-rayfield-file-suit-against-spacex-parent-company-of-x-xai-and-grok-over-creation-and-promotion-of-child-exploitation-and-non-consensual-images-on-platform (not reachable from this session); details via search excerpts of coverage such as https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/oregon-virginia-spacex-grok-deepfake-lawsuits.html , https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/11/23/us-states-sue-spacex-over-grok-ais-creation-of-child-sexual-abuse-material and https://www.forbes.com/sites/saradorn/2026/11/23/oregon-and-virginia-sue-spacex-alleging-groks-spicy-mode-allowed-pornographic-deepfakes/ [TODO: verify figures against the complaints]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: industry news / lawsuit. Not taken: AI discourse, conceptual explainer (and not ai-safety-post-crafter: this is about product safeguards and consumer law, not alignment or evals). Angle: frame-shift. The lurid details will lead coverage. The legal theory ("you said it was safe") is the part every AI product team should notice.

Two US states have sued an AI company over what its image tool produced, and they didn't need a new AI law to do it. Virginia and Oregon are using the same consumer-protection rules that cover misleading ads.

On Nov 23, Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones and Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield filed separate lawsuits against SpaceX, the social platform X, and SpaceX Super Intelligence (the Grok maker formerly called xAI). Both center on Grok's "spicy mode," which the suits say let users take a photo of a real person, including children, and turn it into a nude or sexual image or video. The complaints cite a study finding Grok generated about 3 million sexualized images in under two weeks, with roughly 23,000 appearing to depict children [TODO: verify figures against the complaints]. The states want civil penalties, damages and court orders. SpaceX hadn't responded to requests for comment when the suits were filed.

What caught my attention is the legal theory. The states' central claim is about what the company said: that it presented Grok and X as safe while shipping a feature built to produce exactly this, which they call a deceptive business practice. In an earlier suit by Baltimore, xAI leaned on Section 230 (the US law that mostly shields platforms from liability for what users post) [TODO: verify]. My guess, and it's only a guess, is that a "you misled consumers" claim is harder to answer with that defense, because it targets the company's own product choices and statements.

To be fair to the other side, Musk's companies have consistently put the blame on the users who made the images, and none of this has been tested in court. Two lawsuits are allegations, not findings.

If they hold up, every company shipping a generative AI feature should expect its safety marketing to be read back to it in court.
