## 1. Jensen Huang's first-ever tweet had 30+ signatures on it. The two missing names are the interesting part.

**Source:** https://fortune.com/2026/07/24/jensen-huang-open-source-letter-nvidia-kimi/ (cross-referenced: CNBC, Tom's Hardware, TechRadar, The Register, BigGo Finance)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / discourse — frame-shift: most coverage led with "Jensen Huang posted on X for the first time," treating the post itself as the story; the sharper story is the signatory list, who signed and who conspicuously didn't.

---

Jensen Huang posted on X for the first time this week, and every outlet led with that. The sharper story is a few paragraphs down: who actually signed the letter he was sharing.

The letter, "Open Weights and American AI Leadership," argues that open-weight models (AI systems whose underlying settings anyone can download, inspect, and modify, rather than only reach through someone else's app) need to stay legal and unrestricted alongside closed models like GPT or Claude. It arrived four days after reports that the Trump administration is reviving a push to restrict Chinese open-weight models, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 specifically, and just as the White House is separately accusing China of lifting training techniques from Anthropic.

By Friday evening the signatory count had climbed past 30: Nvidia, Microsoft, Meta, Dell, IBM, Palantir, Hugging Face, Cisco, GitHub, and more, with OpenAI added to the list later that same day, per the version Microsoft published. Anthropic and Google did not sign.

I don't read that split as coincidence. A ban on Chinese open-weight models mostly protects whoever doesn't depend on open weights to compete, and that's close to a list of who's missing here. Anthropic and Google both sell closed models as their core product. Nvidia sells chips regardless of which kind of model runs on them, Meta's Llama strategy needs open weights to stay legal to distribute, and Hugging Face's entire business is hosting them. The signers and the two most conspicuous absences line up almost exactly with whose revenue needs open weights to keep existing.

That doesn't make the letter wrong. Open-weight models really do let more outside researchers audit, red-team, and build on frontier AI than closed ones allow. But a lobbying letter is also a map of who has something at stake in the outcome, and this one drew that map unusually clearly.

## 2. DeepSeek just became the first major AI lab to charge rush-hour prices for tokens

**Source:** https://deepseek.ai/blog/deepseek-v4-ga-surge-pricing-migration [TODO: verify — could not fetch this URL directly; content reconstructed from cross-referenced secondary coverage] (cross-referenced: MorphLLM, BenchLM.ai, TechNode, Developers Digest, Tech Buzz China)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / API economics — frame-shift: coverage frames this as "DeepSeek V4 launches"; the model has effectively been available since April/July 20, the actual July 24 news is the legacy-API cutover and the debut of peak/off-peak surge pricing.

---

If you build on DeepSeek's API, the same prompt can now cost you twice as much depending on what time you send it.

That's the actual news underneath the "DeepSeek V4 launches" headlines this week. The V4 models themselves, V4-Pro and V4-Flash, have technically been generally available since around July 20. What changed on July 24 is enforcement: DeepSeek killed the old shortcut model names, `deepseek-chat` and `deepseek-reasoner`, that had been quietly forwarding requests to V4 under the hood. Every app still calling those old names started getting errors instead of answers. Baked into that migration is something I haven't seen a major AI lab do before: peak-hour surge pricing. Call the API during Beijing business hours (9 to noon and 2 to 6 pm) and you pay double what you'd pay at 2am. Off-peak, V4-Pro runs $0.435 per million input tokens and $0.87 per million output tokens; during peak hours, both prices double.

DeepSeek isn't hiding the ball here. It emailed developers about this back in late June, so July 24 is the day the change started actually biting anyone who hadn't migrated, not the day the policy was invented.

The capability side still holds up. Third-party trackers put V4-Pro at 80.6% on SWE-bench Verified, a benchmark that checks whether a model can fix real reported bugs in real GitHub repositories. That ties Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro and lands within 0.2 points of Claude Opus 4.6, for roughly a seventh of Opus's price per token. I haven't independently verified those exact numbers myself, so treat the decimal points as approximate rather than gospel. [TODO: verify exact SWE-bench figures against an independent leaderboard]

What interests me more than the benchmark parity is what the pricing implies. A lab doesn't introduce time-of-day surcharges for fun. It does it because GPU capacity is genuinely tight at certain hours, and letting demand go unpriced during those windows means rationing by outage instead of by cost. If DeepSeek needs peak pricing to manage its own compute, "AI inference keeps getting cheaper" was never the full story. It is cheaper on average, with real constraints showing up right at the edges.

## 3. A tiny router just claimed to beat Anthropic's best model without touching it

**Source:** https://sakana.ai/fugu-release/ (cross-referenced: the-decoder, MarkTechPost, Sakana's original Fugu technical report on arXiv)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Technique / pattern — niche concept made legible: "beating a frontier model" usually means being a bigger, better-trained model; Fugu-Ultra claims to do it purely through smarter orchestration of other companies' models, with none of the numbers independently verified yet.

---

Sakana AI says its Fugu-Ultra v1.1 now beats Anthropic's Fable 5 model on aggregate benchmarks. Fable 5 isn't even one of the models Fugu is allowed to use.

That sentence sounds like a typo, so it's worth explaining what Fugu actually is. Behind the scenes it isn't one model at all, it's several other companies' models wired together by a small coordinator, about 0.6 billion parameters, tiny by frontier standards, that decides how to split each incoming request across a pool of larger models. The coordinator wasn't trained the normal way either. Instead of gradient descent (the standard method of nudging a model's weights toward better answers), Sakana evolved it with a genetic-algorithm-style method called CMA-ES, closer to breeding generations of coordinators and keeping the ones that route well than teaching one coordinator directly. It assigns each model in the pool a role, Thinker, Worker, or Verifier, and reshuffles those roles turn by turn rather than committing to one model per request.

The v1.1 update, announced July 24, claims up to 7.9 points of improvement over the original release from June, with the biggest jumps on ProgramBench and TerminalBench 2.1 (two coding and terminal-agent benchmarks). Pricing hasn't moved: still $5 per million input tokens, $30 per million output. I want to flag clearly that every number here, the 7.9-point gain, the win over Fable 5, all of it, comes from Sakana's own announcement. Nobody outside the company has verified it yet. Even taking that with appropriate skepticism, the shape of the claim is the interesting part. If a coordinator can out-orchestrate a frontier model without including that model in its pool, that's evidence that some of what looks like "capability" is actually about knowing which model to ask and when, not about which model is biggest. I don't think this replaces training better models. But it's a genuinely different lever, and Sakana pulling it twice in five weeks, first in June and again this week, suggests they think there's more room in that lever than most labs are betting on.
