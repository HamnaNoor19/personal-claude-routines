## 1. A Fields medalist let AI agents loose on his 27-year-old teaching code — and they found bugs he didn't know were there

**Source:** https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/07/11/old-and-new-apps-via-modern-coding-agents/
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** AI discourse / hands-on observation report — frame-shift: this is being read as "AI helps a genius code faster," but the more specific and more useful finding is that thorough translation work doubles as a bug audit.

---

Terence Tao, one of the most decorated living mathematicians, spent part of last week letting AI coding agents loose on code he wrote in 1999. The write-ups framing this as another "AI helps genius" story are missing the more specific thing he actually reported.

Tao had about two dozen small teaching applets, built in an old version of Java for a complex analysis course, that stopped working years ago when browsers dropped support for that runtime. He had an agent port them to JavaScript. In translating the old code line by line, the agent surfaced two bugs in Tao's original 1999 code that he says he never knew were there, alongside introducing one new bug of its own, a mishandled drag event in one applet. By his own count, that's roughly a wash on code quality, not a clean win for either side.

What stands out more to me is what happened next. Back in 1999, Tao had wanted to build a visualization tool for special relativity, something he described then as "Inkscape, but in Minkowski space" [TODO: verify exact quote wording], and shelved it because it was too much work. With an agent doing the implementation, he had a working version, complete with relativistic Doppler coloring and a live backward-light-cone view, in a couple of hours.

Porting old code is a mundane task. My read is that doing it thoroughly enough to actually rewrite each function meant the agent effectively re-derived the underlying logic, and re-deriving logic is exactly the kind of process that turns up bugs nobody was looking for. That's a narrower and more interesting claim than "AI writes better code than a Fields medalist."

Worth being honest about scope here. This is one researcher's account of applets written for teaching, not production code, and not a benchmark anyone else can reproduce. "Found two legacy bugs, introduced one" is Tao's own tally, not an independently audited count.

## 2. OpenAI's safety team lost its independent reporting line — and its sixth leader in two years

**Source:** https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-11/openai-safety-head-heidecke-to-leave-firm-after-reshuffle-wired (cross-referenced: Engadget, TechTimes, PYMNTS, TheNextWeb, Technobezz, Yahoo Tech)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Governance / policy — frame-shift: coverage reads this as one more executive exit; the structural story is that safety review lost the independent reporting line that let it push back on launch decisions.

---

OpenAI's head of safety, Johannes Heidecke, is leaving the company by July 24, and the reason matters more than the departure itself. Chief research officer Mark Chen told staff in an internal memo this week that the safety systems team, the group that reviews models and products before they ship, no longer operates as its own function. It now reports up through the research organization, to Mia Glaese, whose title expands to VP of research and safety. Saachi Jain steps in as interim head of safety systems in the meantime.

Coverage of this is mostly running it as one more executive exit, and Heidecke is indeed the sixth senior safety leader to leave OpenAI in roughly two years, after Jan Leike, Miles Brundage, Steven Adler, Andrea Vallone, and chief futurist Joshua Achiam. But the personnel churn is the smaller story. The structural one is that the team responsible for flagging a launch as risky now sits inside the organization it's supposed to be able to say no to, rather than beside it.

Chen's stated reason is speed. He wrote that safety needs "an earlier and more direct role in shaping key model, product, and launch decisions" [TODO: verify exact quote wording], because release cycles have compressed and models now train on a faster cadence than before. That's a real pressure, and folding review earlier into development is a defensible response to it, not obviously a bad-faith one.

What's worth sitting with is this: independent safety functions exist in industries like aviation and pharma precisely because a reviewer who answers to the same manager who owns the ship date tends to lose leverage over time, even when both sides act in good faith. Nobody can say yet whether that dynamic plays out here. What's confirmed is that the org chart changed and the person who ran the old structure is out. What's still open is whether the new one catches what the old one would have.

## 3. A senator's new AI bill package skips model capability entirely and targets data centers and hospital overrides instead

**Source:** https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/senator-markey-releases-the-ai-accountability-agenda-taking-power-back-from-big-tech (cross-referenced: MLex, TheNextWeb, Congress.gov bill text)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / regulation — stakes-first: most AI policy fights are about what a model can do; this one is about what gets built near you and what it's allowed to decide for you.

---

Most AI regulation debate in Washington is about model capability: what a frontier system can do, how it's evaluated before release. Senator Ed Markey's new proposal, released this week, is aimed somewhere else entirely: the data centers that power AI, and the decisions AI is allowed to make about you once it's built.

Markey bundled close to a dozen bills into what his office is calling an "AI Accountability Agenda." Two pieces stand out. The AI Environmental Impacts Act would require AI data centers to report their environmental and energy impact, hand that measurement job to a NIST-convened standards group, and fine operators that don't comply. The Right to Override Act would require hospitals and health plans to give patients and clinicians a way to overrule an AI's recommendation, and protect health workers from retaliation for disagreeing with one.

Neither piece is new by itself. Both are reintroductions of bills Markey has floated in earlier sessions [TODO: verify prior-session bill numbers], and neither has cleared committee yet. What's different is bundling them under one banner, at a moment when data center buildout is the most physically visible part of the AI boom in a lot of communities, and AI-assisted decisions are already showing up in hospital workflows without much public rule-making around them.

I don't think this agenda passes intact. Divided control of Congress and industry lobbying make that a long shot for any single bill here, let alone a dozen bundled together. What I'd watch instead is which of the dozen gets picked off and attached to something that does move, because the environmental-reporting piece in particular addresses something regulators currently have almost no visibility into: how much power and water a given AI buildout actually uses once it's running.
