## 1. OpenAI buried its next model's announcement in paragraph three of a math blog post

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/ten-advances-in-mathematics/ (cross-referenced with Forbes, BleepingComputer, TheNextWeb, the-decoder, BigGo Finance)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Model release / capability evaluation — frame-shift: the story isn't that OpenAI's next model is good at math, it's that the claim comes in a form you can check yourself instead of taking on faith.

---

OpenAI announced its next major model, Astra, in the third paragraph of a blog post about math. Not a keynote, not a product page. A math writeup, with the actual news buried past the fold.

What that buried announcement is doing is worth unpacking. An internal version of Astra just resolved ten problems that had sat unsolved in pure math and theoretical computer science for years, in some cases decades. The headline one: mathematicians have wondered since 1999 whether every countable group can be approximated by finite systems, a property called "sofic," after the mathematician who posed the question. Astra produced the first explicit example of a group that can't be, closing a question that had stood for 27 years. Separately, it found the first improvement since 1978 to how tightly spheres can be packed in high dimensions.

The part that should change how you read this isn't the results, it's the format. Every argument was formalized in Lean, a proof assistant that checks each logical step by computer instead of by a human reviewer's judgment call. Nobody has to take OpenAI's word for whether these proofs hold. Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers, a winner of math's highest honor, reviewed one and said he'd recommend it for a top journal without hesitation, and unlike a benchmark score, that claim is independently checkable by anyone willing to read the Lean code.

Total compute cost across all ten: about $2,000.

Worth being precise about what this isn't. Astra itself isn't public yet. Humans still wrote the papers up from the model's output. And math with a formal verifier is close to the easiest domain for this to work in, most claims an AI model makes on the job won't come with a Lean certificate attached. But the gap between "trust our eval" and "check the proof yourself" is real, and it's rare to see a capability claim close it this cleanly.

## 2. Two Korean labs shipped near-identical 700B open models 48 hours apart, days before a government elimination round

**Source:** https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322610/20260801/open-source-700b-models-released-two-days-apart-korea-nears-sovereign-ai-elimination-round.htm (cross-referenced with Korea Times, aibase, The Elec)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / business — frame-shift: coverage is reading this as "another open model that matches the frontier," the sharper story is a government literally about to eliminate one of the teams building it.

---

South Korea's government is about to cut its national AI project from four teams to three. Two of those teams just shipped near-identical 700-billion-parameter open models within 48 hours of each other.

On July 29, SK Telecom put A.X K2, a 688-billion-parameter model, on Hugging Face, the site where AI labs share trained model weights for anyone to download and run. Two days later, on July 31, LG AI Research followed with K-EXAONE 2.0 at 750 billion parameters. Both are Mixture of Experts models, an architecture that splits the network into specialist sub-networks and activates only a handful per input, which is how a model this large stays affordable to run. Both use the same 262,144-token context window, roughly how much text the model can hold in mind at once, and the same permissive Apache 2.0 license that lets any company use or modify the weights commercially. Evaluators convene August 8 to 11 to decide which of the four government-backed teams survive.

K-EXAONE 2.0's numbers are real, not just bigger-is-better bragging. It averages 70.1 across 24 benchmarks spanning nine categories, up from 63.3 for LG's prior model. On one long-context retrieval test, OpenAI's MRCR, which checks whether a model can still find the right needle in a very long haystack of text, it scored 94.4, ahead of Alibaba's Qwen 3.5 at 93.0 and DeepSeek's V4 Pro Max at 92.9.

What's easy to miss inside the "another open model that matches the frontier" framing is how unusual the setup around it is. This isn't two companies racing in a market and letting revenue sort out the winner. It's two teams racing toward a government evaluation panel that will formally eliminate one of them from a national program, and both chose to converge on nearly the same architecture, context length, and license rather than differentiate. That's a more legible form of AI competition than we normally get to see from outside a lab.

## 3. A Fields Medalist just chose AI safety over the exact kind of math breakthrough OpenAI shipped this week

**Source:** Wall Street Journal profile by Ben Cohen, via Techmeme https://www.techmeme.com/260802/p5 (cross-referenced with BetaKit, AI Weekly, remio.ai)
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Alignment research (not taken: governance/policy, capability evals) — angle: frame-shift on what he chose over what, landing one day after Astra's proof-verified math result.

---

Jacob Tsimerman won math's highest prize a week and a half ago. He announced at the ceremony that he's leaving academia to join OpenAI, not to push AI further into math, but to work on AI safety.

The prize is the Fields Medal, awarded every four years to mathematicians under 40 and generally treated as the field's Nobel. Tsimerman won it for reshaping a corner of number theory called o-minimality into a tool that helped prove the André-Oort conjecture, a decades-old open question about points on certain geometric spaces. He's the second Canadian to win it, ever.

What he said at the podium is the more interesting part. Tsimerman told the room he believes AI systems will soon outperform human mathematicians at the actual work of mathematics, and that mathematicians need to get involved in how those systems are built now, while there's still a chance to shape how they reason, not after. He's taking a leave from the University of Toronto rather than resigning, and by his own account has no fixed plan past the next year.

It's worth sitting with what he chose over what. OpenAI spent this past week publicizing exactly the kind of result Tsimerman is talking about: an internal model that resolved ten open math problems with proofs formally checked by a computer rather than trusted on the model's word alone (see item 1). That's the demonstration of AI-does-math. Tsimerman had a front-row seat to go work on making that better, and chose safety instead. I don't know his reasoning beyond what he said publicly, but the juxtaposition is hard to miss: the same week AI showed it can produce a mathematical claim you can verify line by line, one of the mathematicians best equipped to judge that claim chose to spend his time on whether the systems producing it can be trusted, not on making them better at producing it.

Worth flagging what isn't settled here. This is one researcher's bet, not a finding, and "AI safety" covers a wide range of actual work, from technical alignment research to policy, and it's not yet public which of those Tsimerman will do [TODO: verify]. Mathematicians are reportedly split on whether this is a serious use of his time or a loss to the field, and that disagreement is worth taking seriously rather than resolving from the outside.
