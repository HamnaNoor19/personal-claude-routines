## 1. OpenAI parts ways with three safety researchers over alleged information-sharing

The people hired to check whether a powerful AI system is safe are, by definition, the people who see inside it. This week OpenAI parted ways with three of them.

According to reporting from the Wall Street Journal (relayed by TechCrunch on Oct 1), Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni were let go after an internal review concluded they shared confidential information with an outside AI-safety organization. Bloomberg reportedly says the material concerned infrastructure architecture. In the other, a company removed people who were pressing on safety, and used a confidentiality rule to do it. I can't tell which this is from public reporting, and I'd distrust anyone who claims they can.

What I can say is that the timing is awkward. The New York Times recently reported that OpenAI brushed aside employee warnings about safety practices when testing models. [TODO: verify] Even if both stories have innocent explanations, the combination leaves outsiders with no good way to check.

That is the structural problem. Safety oversight inside a lab depends on employees who can speak up without losing their jobs, and on clear, trusted channels for doing it. Which of those failed here is the question I'd want answered.

## 2. Microsoft: attackers now weaponize new vulnerabilities in under a day, defenders take 30-60 days
**Source:** https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/security-insider/threat-landscape/2026-digital-defense-report (via search summaries; full report not fetched) [TODO: verify numbers against the report]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter (security frame)
**Frame / angle:** AI security (adversaries using AI, not model misbehavior). Stakes-first: a number gap that anyone running software can feel.

Here is a gap worth knowing about if your company runs any software at all: attackers can now turn a newly found flaw into a working attack in well under 24 hours, while a typical large company takes 30 to 60 days to patch a serious one.

Both figures come from Microsoft's 2026 Digital Defense Report, published this week. The "under 24 hours" number is a median, and Microsoft attributes the compression largely to AI, describing a shift from needing human experts to, in many cases, simply writing a prompt. It also projects a record roughly 72,000 publicly tracked vulnerabilities (CVEs) for 2026. [TODO: verify all three figures]

A framing note, since safety and security get blurred: this is a security story. Nothing here says the models are misbehaving. It says people with bad intentions are using ordinary AI capability to move faster, and it is the attackers' side of the ledger that is growing quickest.

The part I found most useful is Microsoft's explanation of why defenders can't just match the speed. Production systems need testing before a fix ships, and AI doesn't remove that constraint. So the bottleneck is a boring organizational one, not a missing tool.

One caution: the report comes from a vendor that sells security products, and a median hides a lot of spread. I'd read it as a strong directional signal, not a precise measurement.

I don't have an answer for how a 30-day process survives a 1-day adversary. I'd like to see which fixes actually shorten that loop.

## 3. Anthropic opens Claude Code to "mods": small TypeScript programs that can rewrite the agent's behavior
**Source:** https://postcutoff.com/e/2026-10-01-claude-code-mods/ (secondary coverage; Anthropic's own docs not fetched, egress/time) [TODO: verify against Anthropic announcement]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Product launch / dev tooling. Stakes-first: the extension point is also the attack surface, and it ships on by default with no sandbox.

If you use Claude Code, a new kind of add-on can now sit between you and the agent, read what it's about to do, and change it. Anthropic calls them mods.

Launched Oct 1 (requires Claude Code v2.1.287 or later, on by default), a mod is a small TypeScript function that hooks into the agent's events. Per the coverage, it can rewrite your prompt before the model sees it, block or retry a tool call, approve or deny a permission request, redact secrets from output, or replace parts of the interface. Anthropic also rebuilt three of its own features (the diff pane, the agents.md loader and telemetry) as mods, which is a good sign they intend this to be load-bearing. [TODO: verify]

The generous reading: this is what a mature developer tool does. Editors and browsers became ecosystems through exactly this kind of door, and a team that wants every command checked for secrets can now do it in code.

The detail I keep returning to is that mods reportedly run in the same process as Claude Code, with your machine's access and no sandbox. [TODO: verify] A mod that decides permissions is a mod that can silently approve things. Several mods run as a chain in registration order, so one hostile entry could change what the rest see. None of this is a flaw in the idea, but it means installing a mod is closer to installing a program than flipping a setting.

I haven't tried writing one yet. My plan is to read the first few community mods before running any.
