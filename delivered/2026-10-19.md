## 1. Schwab and JPMorgan are blocking outside AI agents from moving customer money
**Source:** https://thenextweb.com/news/schwab-blocks-ai-agents-meta-muse (secondary; Bloomberg TV interview with Schwab CEO Rick Wurster not located, [TODO: verify]. Also: https://www.americanbanker.com/news/schwab-blocks-unauthorized-ai-agents-points-clients-to-charley)
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news. Stakes-first: the first real "no" from finance to consumer AI agents, and the unanswered liability question underneath it.

Format: LinkedIn post.

Schwab's CEO says he now gets a weekly report on AI agents his firm has blocked from moving client money. The count for the big ones: "tens and twenties" a week.

If you've ever thought about handing an AI assistant your banking login so it can pay bills or move cash for you, this is the first big institutional answer: not like that. Schwab says it is blocking agents (Meta's Muse is named in the coverage) that try to act using a customer's credentials or by driving a browser the way a person would. JPMorgan said on its third-quarter earnings call that it blocks outside agents from acting for retail customers too. Read-only access, like a budgeting app seeing your balance through Plaid, stays allowed.

What I find interesting is the alternative Schwab is offering. It plans to set up MCP servers (a standard plug that lets AI tools ask a company's systems for specific things) where an outside assistant can check balances and pull research but cannot send money. So the line being drawn is between seeing and doing, not between AI and no AI.

My read, and I might be wrong: this is less about security theater and more about liability. Wurster's own question was who pays if an agent makes a mistake, and as far as I can tell there is no settled US answer yet. Until there is, a bank has every reason to be the one holding the keys.

I'm working from secondary coverage here, not the Bloomberg interview itself, so treat the exact quotes as provisional.

## 2. Stanford review: of 800+ AI-in-K-12 papers, only 20 have strong causal evidence, none from US schools
**Source:** https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education (report: "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review", Fesler, Martinez Claeys, Agnew, Loeb). Hook is a reported coalition of 140+ nonprofits asking Congress for funding: [TODO: verify], could not find the letter. **Recency flag:** the report itself appears to date from March 2026 [TODO: verify]; only the Congress push is reported as current.
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Industry news / evidence check. Skeptical-of-hype: schools are buying AI tools on a very thin evidence base.

Format: LinkedIn post.

Out of more than 800 studies on AI in K-12 classrooms, a Stanford review found 20 that could actually show AI caused a learning change. Zero of those were in US K-12 schools.

That's from Stanford SCALE's "Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review" (Fesler, Martinez Claeys, Agnew, Loeb), and it's the number I keep coming back to as districts sign AI contracts. "Causal" is the key word. Most of the 800 can tell you students liked a tool or scored well while using it. Only a study with a proper comparison (a control group, or something equivalent) can say the tool is why. The authors report that much of the strong work involves older learners, happens outside the US, and tests short-term outcomes, sometimes a single 20-minute session.

I should be careful about what this does and doesn't show. It doesn't show AI tutoring fails. It shows that the question has barely been tested where decisions are being made. Absence of evidence, not evidence of absence, and I'd say that to anyone who wanted to read it as a takedown.

Reportedly, a coalition of more than 140 nonprofits is now asking Congress to fund this research. If that's accurate, it's the right ask. A school buying a tool today is making a bet that nobody has measured yet, and that seems worth saying plainly.

## 3. Anthropic's update on independent evaluators: who should pay the people checking the lab?
**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news (post "An update on independent evaluators", Oct 15, 2026; could not open the post itself, 404/unreachable, so details below are from secondary coverage, [TODO: verify]). Coverage: https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-is-paying-the-firm-that-will-evaluate-it-and-says-in-the-same-announcement-that-this-is-not-how-it-should-work
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Governance / policy. Frame-shift: the interesting part is the lab saying the arrangement it just made isn't how it should work. **Recency flag:** Oct 15 update (4 days old) building on the Sep 18 Accenture announcement; marginal against the 48h bar.

Frame: governance. Angle: frame-shift.

Anthropic is paying the firm that will evaluate it, and said in the same announcement that this isn't how it should work. I can't remember a lab being that direct about a conflict of interest in its own setup.

The background, per secondary coverage: in September Anthropic named Accenture as its first "embedded evaluator", outside staff with employee-like access who test new models for dangerous behavior. Reported commitment is at least $1 billion from each side over five years [TODO: verify]. Anthropic's stated worry is that there are, in its words, no standards yet for what embedded evaluators can see or how they report findings, and that long-term funding should come from pooled industry or government money, which doesn't exist yet.

Here's the opposing view, and I think it's fair: deep access is exactly what makes an evaluation worth anything. Testing from outside, with a few days of API access, misses things an insider would catch. If that's the price of real access, someone has to pay it.

But the critics' comparison sticks with me. Financial auditors are barred from holding a financial stake in their clients precisely because the person paying shapes what gets said. Reporting also says the question of what evaluators may publish without Anthropic's sign-off is still contested, and sources disagree on how much freedom the tiers give them. That publishing right is where I'd look first, since an evaluator who can't speak freely is closer to a consultant than a check.

I haven't read the October 15 post itself, so this rests on press accounts of it.
