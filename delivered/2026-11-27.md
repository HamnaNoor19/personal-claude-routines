## 1. AI shopping agents bought an obviously fake deal 37% of the time, and often after flagging it as suspicious
**Source:** No Hacks test write-up, "AI Shoppers Bought an Obviously Fake Deal 37% of the Time," https://nohacks.co/blog/ai-shoppers-bought-an-obviously-fake-deal ; coverage incl. https://dataconomy.com/2026/11/27/ai-shopping-agents-chose-fake-deals-37-of-the-time/ (neither reachable from this session; figures via search excerpts) [TODO: verify all figures against the No Hacks write-up]
**Skill used:** ai-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Format: LinkedIn post. Frame: capability evaluation (not product launch, not hands-on). Angle: stakes-first, built around the gap between noticing a red flag and acting on it.

Give an AI shopping agent a store shelf with one obviously fake listing, a perfect 5.0 rating and a price far below everything else, and across nine models it bought the fake about 37% of the time.

That figure comes from a test by No Hacks, published this week: 1,202 fake picks out of 3,240 purchase attempts on a mocked-up Christmas shopping site. [TODO: verify denominator; coverage disagrees on whether 37% is of 3,240 first-run attempts or tied to 6,156 total trips]

The number I keep coming back to is smaller. The models described a deal as suspicious 315 times, and still bought it in 137 of those cases. Spotting the red flag and acting on it turned out to be two separate skills.

Where the fake sat on the shelf mattered a lot. Listed first, it got picked 65% of the time. Telling the agent to find the "best value" pushed the rate to 53%, against 19% when no preference was given, and "find me the best value" is roughly the instruction most of us would actually give a shopping assistant.

Results also swung widely inside a single model family. Claude Opus never took the bait in 360 tries, while the smaller Claude Haiku and Sonnet models were reported at 60% and 57%, the highest in the test. [TODO: verify model versions and per-model rates] Choosing a cheaper, faster model to run an agent is usually treated as a cost decision. In this test it was also a judgment decision.

Fair caveats: this is one synthetic shop with six products, run over two days (Nov 14 and 15) by a single tester, not peer reviewed, and not a fraud rate for real stores. My read is that it's still worth knowing before handing an agent a card this holiday season. The agent may describe the scam to you accurately and then buy it anyway.

## 2. UK MPs ask Anthropic for the parts of an outside safety review it blacked out, with a December 4 deadline
**Source:** Letter from Liam Byrne MP, chair of the UK Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee, to Anthropic, dated Nov 27, 2026, via coverage incl. https://runtimewire.com/article/uk-committee-seeks-unredacted-metr-report-from-anthropic-asks-for-december-4 and https://snapereport.com/s/uk-mps-ask-anthropic-for-unredacted-metr-ai-rd-acceleration-report ; METR's statement on the redactions: https://x.com/METR_Evals/status/2118412194702598339 (none reachable from this session; details via search excerpts) [TODO: verify against the committee's published letter]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: governance / policy (not capability evals; the underlying METR report was drafted Nov 9, so this post is about the oversight event, not the findings). Angle: stakes-first, with a fair hearing for Anthropic's reason to redact.

A UK parliamentary committee has asked Anthropic to hand over the parts of an independent safety review that Anthropic removed before it was published. The committee wants a reply by December 4.

The review is METR's assessment, published Nov 5, of how much AI is speeding up Anthropic's own research. METR is an outside group that tests frontier AI models. Its public estimate was that Anthropic's models are improving on well-defined tasks at roughly twice the pace seen from March 2024 to October 2025, with AI itself driving most of that speed-up. [TODO: verify ~2x vs ~1.9x and the ~80% attribution] METR also said, unusually plainly, that Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence METR found most informative, so the public "must rely on our judgment more than we consider ideal."

On Nov 27, Liam Byrne, who chairs the Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee, wrote asking for the full report or the omitted material, plus an explanation of the redactions.

Anthropic's reason for the cuts deserves a fair hearing. It says the removed material is confidential and could help other developers move faster. If your worry is AI development racing ahead, that is not a silly concern; publishing a recipe for speeding up AI research in order to prove that AI research is speeding up has an obvious tension in it.

What the letter really tests is bigger than this one report. The UK's AI Security Institute works with labs by agreement and has no legal power to make them hand anything over. Outside reviews like METR's are one of the few ways people outside a lab learn what is happening inside one. If the lab decides which parts of its reviewer's evidence the public sees, the review is only as credible as both parties, not just the reviewer.

For now, the only fact is that a committee asked. Whether Parliament can compel anything, and whether Anthropic shares the material privately, publicly or not at all, is still open. I'd watch the December 4 reply more closely than the letter.

## 3. Scott Aaronson says AI labs are sitting on unpublished "bombshells." The encryption-breaking rumor attached to it is a separate, unvouched-for claim
**Source:** Scott Aaronson, Shtetl-Optimized blog post, Nov 25, 2026 (exact post title and URL not found; blog at https://scottaaronson.blog/), plus a Nov 24 comment; coverage incl. https://computing.net/scott-aaronson-says-ai-labs-hold-secret-results-that-could-break-encryption/ and https://dealroom.co/news/175858-ai-labs-likely-sitting-on-crypto-bombshells-aaronson-says/ ; Anthropic's Nov 16 disclosure-policy update (fetched and verified): https://www.anthropic.com/research/coordinated-vulnerability-disclosure-timelines [TODO: verify Aaronson quotes against his post; all quotes are from coverage]
**Skill used:** ai-safety-post-crafter
**Frame / angle:** Frame: AI security (not alignment, not dangerous-capability evals). Angle: skeptical pushback on the RSA hype, pivoting to the disclosure gap that exists whether or not the rumor is true.

Scott Aaronson, a prominent computer scientist at UT Austin who previously worked at OpenAI, wrote this week that he is "virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources" that AI labs are sitting on unpublished "bombshells." The rumor spreading alongside it, that a lab has found a fast way to break the encryption behind much of the internet, is a different claim, and he hasn't vouched for it.

Coverage keeps merging those two, so it's worth pulling them apart. In his Nov 25 post, Aaronson said he is much less confident about what the bombshells actually are until he sees details. A day earlier he had mentioned "many, many rumors" about factoring (splitting huge numbers into primes, the problem RSA encryption depends on being hard) and warned they may have been distorted in a game of Telephone. Per later reporting, he says what he is sure of falls "well short" of fast factoring. No lab has commented, and nothing has been demonstrated.

Why I still think it's worth a post: the surrounding facts are real. Labs are publishing AI-generated mathematics in bulk, with OpenAI releasing 722 manuscripts in October and Google six AI-written papers on Nov 25 [TODO: verify both counts], and Aaronson relayed in October that labs had begun quietly testing whether their internal models could break cryptography. [TODO: verify; single secondary source]

If a model ever did find a serious weakness in a widely used encryption scheme, who gets told first? For ordinary software bugs there are established norms: tell the vendor privately, give them time to patch, then publish. Anthropic tightened its own version on Nov 16, cutting the window for high-severity bugs from 90 days to 30 from January. Those rules assume there is a vendor who can ship a fix. A broken math assumption sits under thousands of products at once, and the policy's closest provision, a negotiated timeline for flaws that need changes to a technical standard, was written with ordinary bugs in mind.

My read: the specific rumor is probably wrong in the form it's circulating. The disclosure question underneath it is real, and it's better settled before anyone needs the answer.
